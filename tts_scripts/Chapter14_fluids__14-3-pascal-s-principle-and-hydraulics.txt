Pascal's Principle and Hydraulics.

Pascal's Principle.

In 1653, Blaise Pascal published his *Treatise on the Equilibrium of Liquids*, in which he develops his principles of static fluids. When a fluid is not flowing (i.e., static), we say that the fluid is in static equilibrium. If the fluid is water, we say it is in hydrostatic equilibrium. For a fluid in static equilibrium, the net force on any part of the fluid be zero because the fluid will would otherwise start to flow.

Pascal's observations provide the foundation for hydraulics. Pascal observed that a *change in pressure* applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished throughout the fluid and to the walls of the container. **Pascal's principle** implies that the total pressure in a fluid is the sum of the pressures from different sources. 

Note that this principle does not say that the pressure is the *same* at all points of a fluid. A good example is the fluid at a depth depends on the depth of the fluid and the pressure of the atmosphere. Rather, Pascal's principle applies to the *change* in pressure.

Applications of Pascal's Principle and Hydraulic Systems.

Hydraulic systems are used to operate automotive brakes, hydraulic jacks, and many other mechanical systems (see Figure 14.13 ). We can derive a relationship between the forces in a simple hydraulic system by applying Pascal's principle.

The two pistons (in the figure) are at the same height, so there is no difference in pressure due to a difference in depth. The pressure due to F sub 1 action on area A sub 1 is simply

 P sub 1 equals F sub 1 over A sub 1. 

According to Pascal's principle, the pressure is transmitted undiminished throughout the fluid and to all walls of the container. Thus, a pressure P sub 2 is felt at the other piston that is equal to P sub 1 (i.e., P sub 1 equals P sub 2 ). However, P sub 2 equals F sub 2 / A sub 2, for this to be so, we find that

 
 F sub 1 over A sub 1 equals F sub 2 over A sub 2.
 

This equation relates the ratios of force to area in any hydraulic system, provided that the pistons are at the same vertical height and that friction in the system is negligible.

Hydraulic systems can increase or decrease the force applied to them. To mak the force larger, te pressure is applied to a larger area. Hydraulic systems are analogous to simple levers, but they have the advantage tha pressure can be sent through curved lines to several places at once.

The **hydraulic jack** is used to lift heavy loads, such as those used by auto mechanics to raise an automobile. It consists of an incompressible fluid filled in a U-tube fitted with a movable piston on each side. One side of the U-tube is narrower than the other. A small force applied over a small area can balance a much larger force on the other side over a larger area (see Figure ).

where F sub 1 is the force applied to lift the car, A sub 1 is the cross-sectional area of the smaller piston, A sub 2 is the cross-sectional area of the larger piston, and F sub 2 is the weight of the car.

The Problem.

A force of 500 newtons is exerted on the pedal cylinder. Pressure created in the pedal cylinder is transmitted to the four wheel cylinders. The Model.

We treat the brake fluid as an ideal fluid in static equilibrium, so Pascal’s principle applies. The pressure is transmitted equally throughout the fluid, which means the pressure in the master cylinder equals the pressure in each wheel cylinder. If F sub 1 is the force applied to the pedal cylinder, and A sub 1 and A sub 2 are the cross-sectional areas of the pedal and wheel cylinders, respectively, then F sub 1 / A sub 1 equals F sub 2 / A sub 2. Because the given 500 newtons is already the force exerted on the pedal cylinder, the lever analysis is not needed here.

---

The Math.

We begin with Pascal’s principle, which states that the pressure is the same in both cylinders. This gives the relationship F sub 1 / A sub 1 equals F sub 2 / A sub 2, where F sub 1 is the force applied to the pedal cylinder and F sub 2 is the force exerted by each wheel cylinder. Solving this equation for the output force gives F sub 2 equals ( A sub 2 / A sub 1 ) F sub 1.

Because both cylinders have circular cross sections, their areas can be written in terms of their radii as A equals pi r squared. Substituting this into the area ratio shows that the factor pi cancels, leaving A sub 2 / A sub 1 equals r sub 2 to the power of 2 / r sub 1 to the power of 2. This allows us to express the force entirely in terms of the cylinder radii.

The given diameters of the pedal and wheel cylinders are 0.500 cm and 2.50 cm, respectively, so the corresponding radii are r sub 1 equals 0.250 cm and r sub 2 equals 1.25 cm. Substituting these values into the force relation gives

 
 F sub 2 equals ( r sub 2 to the power of 2 over r sub 1 to the power of 2 ) F sub 1 
 equals ( (1.25 cm ) squared over (0.250 cm ) squared )(500 newtons ) 
 equals 25(500 newtons ) 
 equals 1.25 times ten to the 4 newtons.

The Conclusion.

Each wheel cylinder exerts a force of 1.25 times ten to the 4 newtons. This result is significantly larger than the 500 newtons force applied to the pedal cylinder, which reflects the large ratio of the cylinder areas in the hydraulic system. Because pressure is transmitted equally throughout the fluid, increasing the cross-sectional area of the output cylinder increases the force it exerts.

This amplification of force explains how a driver can apply a relatively small force at the pedal and still generate a much larger braking force at the wheels. However, this increase in force comes at the expense of distance—the wheel cylinders move a smaller distance than the pedal cylinder, so the total work done remains consistent with energy conservation.
