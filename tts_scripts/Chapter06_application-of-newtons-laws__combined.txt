6.1 Solving Newton's Laws

Application of Newton's Laws.

Chapter roadmap.

 

This chapter applies Newton's laws to more realistic situations involving several common forces. We will use free-body diagrams to analyze systems with friction, tension, normal forces, springs, and circular motion, while keeping the connection between force and acceleration at the center of each problem.

As you read, focus on three questions:

Item 1. Which forces act on the object or system?

Item 2. Which direction should the coordinate axes point?

Item 3. What physical condition determines the acceleration or force balance?

These ideas will be used to solve problems involving connected objects, inclined planes, friction, drag, springs, and circular motion.
 

Solving Newton's Laws.

Note.

This chapter combines several tools developed earlier in the course. We use vector components from Chapter 2, acceleration vectors from Chapter 4, and free-body diagrams from Chapter 5 to apply Newton's second law to real systems.
 

Problem-Solving Strategies.

Applying Newton's Laws of Motion.

 

Most problems in this chapter follow the same pattern: choose a system, identify the external forces, turn those forces into components, and apply Newton's second law.

Item 1. **Define the system of interest.** Decide which object or group of objects you are analyzing. Newton's second law uses only the external forces acting on that system.

Item 2. **Choose useful axes.** Pick coordinate axes that make the force components as simple as possible. When motion occurs along a slope, curve, or rope, it is often useful to align one axis with the motion or acceleration.

Item 3. **Draw a free-body diagram.** Draw only the external forces acting on the chosen system. Do not draw velocity, acceleration, or the net force as separate forces.

Item 4. **Write Newton's second law by components.** Start from sum vector F equals m vector a, then write the component equations such as
 
 sum F sub x equals m a sub x, 
 sum F sub y equals m a sub y.
 

Item 5. **Use constraints and known conditions.** Constant velocity means a equals 0. Static equilibrium means the net force is zero. A massless rope has the same tension throughout. A frictionless pulley changes the direction of the tension but not its magnitude.

Item 6. **Solve symbolically before evaluating.** Keep the algebra general as long as possible. Substitute numerical values only after the physical relationships are clear.

Item 7. **Check the result.** The answer should have the correct units, the correct direction, and a physically reasonable size.
 

This strategy is not a recipe for memorizing equations. It is a way to translate a physical situation into Newton's second law. The hardest step is usually deciding what belongs in the free-body diagram and what does not.

Particle Equilibrium.

A particle in equilibrium is one that obey's Newton's 1st law (i.e., the external forces are balanced). Static equilibrium involves objects at rest, and dynamic equilibrium involves objects in motion *without* acceleration.

Checkpoint.

 

If an object is moving at constant velocity, is it in equilibrium? What does that imply about sum vector F?
 

Particle equilibrium is the force version of constant velocity. If the acceleration is zero, Newton's second law becomes sum vector F equals 0. This extends the equilibrium ideas from Newton's first law and the free-body-diagram workflow from Chapter 5.

The Problem.
Consider the traffic light (mass of 15 kg ) suspended from two wires as shown in Figure. Find the tension in each wire, neglecting the masses of the wires.

The Model.

The system of interest is the traffic light. We model the traffic light as a particle in static equilibrium, so its acceleration is zero. We choose + i hat to point horizontally to the right and + j hat to point vertically upward. With this coordinate choice, the left-wire tension vector T _1 points up and to the left, the right-wire tension vector T _2 points up and to the right, and the weight vector w points downward in the - j hat direction.

The left wire makes an angle of 30 degrees with the horizontal, and the right wire makes an angle of 45 degrees with the horizontal. The two wires are treated as massless and inextensible, so each wire exerts a tension force along its own direction. The only forces acting on the traffic light are the two tensions and the weight (similar to the Tension in a Tightrope). Air resistance and motion of the light are neglected.

---

The Math.

For static equilibrium, Newton’s second law requires the net force on the traffic light to be zero. Since the traffic light has no acceleration in either direction,

 
 sum F sub x equals m a sub x equals 0, 
 sum F sub y equals m a sub y equals 0.
 

The forces acting on the traffic light are the left tension vector T _1, the right tension vector T _2, and the weight vector w equals -mg j hat. Resolving the two tension forces into horizontal and vertical components gives the component equations

 
 sum F sub x equals - T sub 1 cos 30 degrees + T sub 2 cos 45 degrees equals 0, 
 sum F sub y equals T sub 1 sin 30 degrees + T sub 2 sin 45 degrees - mg equals 0.
 

The horizontal equation relates the two unknown tensions. We solve this equation for T sub 2 in terms of T sub 1 so that the vertical equation will contain only one unknown tension:

 
- T sub 1 cos 30 degrees + T sub 2 cos 45 degrees equals 0, 
 T sub 2 cos 45 degrees equals T sub 1 cos 30 degrees, 
 T sub 2 equals T sub 1 cos 30 degrees over cos 45 degrees.
 

The vertical equation determines the actual size of the tensions because the vertical components must balance the weight of the traffic light. We substitute the horizontal-equilibrium relation for T sub 2 into the vertical equation and solve for T sub 1:

 
 T sub 1 sin 30 degrees + T sub 2 sin 45 degrees - mg equals 0, 
 T sub 1 sin 30 degrees + ( T sub 1 cos 30 degrees over cos 45 degrees ) sin 45 degrees equals mg, 
 T sub 1 ( sin 30 degrees + cos 30 degrees over cos 45 degrees sin 45 degrees ) equals mg, 
 T sub 1 equals mg over sin 30 degrees + cos 30 degrees over cos 45 degrees sin 45 degrees.
 

The numerical value of T sub 1 follows from the vertical force balance, and then the horizontal force balance gives T sub 2:

 
 T sub 1 equals (15.0 kg )(9.81 meters per second squared ) over sin 30 degrees + cos 30 degrees over cos 45 degrees sin 45 degrees 
 equals 108 newtons, 
 T sub 2 equals T sub 1 cos 30 degrees over cos 45 degrees 
 equals (108 newtons ) cos 30 degrees over cos 45 degrees 
 equals 132 newtons.
 

---

The Conclusion.

The tension in the left wire is T sub 1 equals 108 newtons, and the tension in the right wire is T sub 2 equals 132 newtons. The right wire has the larger tension because its direction is closer to vertical, so it supplies more of the upward force needed to balance the traffic light’s weight. These values satisfy Newton’s second law for static equilibrium because the horizontal components cancel and the vertical components balance the weight.

What is the drag force of the water on the barge resisting the motion?

Figure referenced in the course notes.

Expand the dropdown below to see the worked solution.

 
 
 
 
 

The Model.

The system of interest is the barge. We model the barge as a rigid object translating on the horizontal surface of the water. We choose + i hat to point in the direction of the first tugboat’s force and + j hat to point in the direction of the second tugboat’s force. With this coordinate choice, the tugboat forces are vector F _1 equals F sub 1 i hat and vector F _2 equals F sub 2 j hat. The resultant tugboat force vector F _ app points in the direction of the barge’s observed acceleration. The drag force exerted by the water points opposite the barge’s motion, so it points opposite vector F _ app. The weight of the barge and the buoyant force from the water balance vertically, so they are omitted from the horizontal force model.

---

The Math.

For horizontal motion, Newton’s second law relates the net horizontal force on the barge to the observed acceleration of the barge.

The forces acting on the barge in the horizontal plane are the tugboat forces vector F _1 and vector F _2 and the drag force vector F _D. The tugboat forces combine to form the applied force vector F _ app, and the drag force acts opposite the barge’s motion. Newton’s second law for the horizontal motion is

 
 sum vector F equals m vector a, 
 vector F _ app + vector F _D equals m vector a.
 

The two tugboat forces are perpendicular, so the magnitude and direction of the applied force come from the vector components of vector F _ app. Resolving the applied force into components gives

 
 vector F _ app equals F sub 1 i hat + F sub 2 j hat, 
F_ app equals square root of F sub 1 to the power of 2 + F sub 2 to the power of 2, 
 theta equals tan to the power of -1 ( F sub 2 over F sub 1 ).
 

The applied-force magnitude and direction follow from the perpendicular tugboat-force components by

 
F_ app equals square root of (2.7 times ten to the 5 newtons ) squared + (3.6 times ten to the 5 newtons ) squared 
 equals 4.5 times ten to the 5 newtons, 
 theta equals tan to the power of -1 ( 3.6 times ten to the 5 over 2.7 times ten to the 5 ) 
 equals 53 degrees.
 

Along the direction of motion, the applied force points forward and the drag force points backward. Newton’s second law along this direction gives

 
F_ app - F sub D equals ma.
 

We solve this equation for the drag force magnitude, which yields

 
 F sub D equals F_ app - ma.
 

The drag force follows from Newton’s second law along the direction of motion as

 
 F sub D equals 4.5 times ten to the 5 newtons - (5 times ten to the 6 kg )(7.5 times ten to the minus 2 meters per second squared ) 
 equals 7.5 times ten to the 4 newtons.
 

---

The Conclusion.

The water exerts a drag force with magnitude F sub D equals 7.5 times ten to the 4 newtons. This drag force acts opposite the barge’s motion, while the tugboat resultant points in the direction of motion. The result is consistent with Newton’s second law because the applied tugboat force is larger than the drag force, leaving a net force that accelerates the barge.

The Model.

The system of interest is the person standing on the scale. We model the person as a particle moving vertically with the elevator. We choose + j hat to point upward and - j hat to point downward. With this coordinate choice, the scale force on the person points upward, so vector F _s equals F sub s j hat, and the weight of the person points downward, so vector w equals -mg j hat. The scale reading is the magnitude of the upward contact force F sub s. Air resistance is neglected, and the motion is one-dimensional along the vertical axis.

---

The Math.

For vertical motion, Newton’s second law relates the net vertical force on the person to the person’s vertical acceleration.

The forces acting on the person are the upward scale force vector F _s and the downward weight vector w. Applying Newton’s second law in the vertical direction gives

 
 sum F sub y equals m a sub y, 
 F sub s - mg equals m a sub y.
 

We solve this equation for the scale reading F sub s, which yields

 
 F sub s equals mg + m a sub y, 
 F sub s equals m(g+ a sub y ).
 

This equation shows that the scale reading depends on the vertical acceleration of the person. We now reuse this same equation for both cases.

**(a)** First, we evaluate the case when the elevator accelerates upward at a sub y equals 1.2 meters per second squared. Since the acceleration points in the + j hat direction, a sub y is positive:

 
 F sub s equals m(g+ a sub y ) 
 equals (75.0 kg ) l(9.81 meters per second squared + 1.20 meters per second squared r) 
 equals 826 newtons.
 

**(b)** Then, we evaluate the case when the elevator moves upward at a constant speed of 1 meters per second. Constant speed implies a sub y equals 0, so

 
 F sub s equals m(g+ a sub y ) 
 equals (75.0 kg )(9.81 meters per second squared + 0) 
 equals 736 newtons.
 

---

The Conclusion.

When the elevator accelerates upward at 1.2 meters per second squared, the scale reads F sub s equals 826 newtons. When the elevator moves upward at constant speed, the acceleration is zero and the scale reads F sub s equals 736 newtons. The scale reading is larger during upward acceleration because the scale must exert enough upward force to both balance the person’s weight and produce the upward acceleration.

The Model.

The system consists of two blocks connected by a light, inextensible string over a frictionless, massless pulley. Block m sub 1 moves horizontally on a frictionless table, and block m sub 2 moves vertically. The string constrains the blocks to have the same acceleration magnitude.

For block m sub 1, we choose + i hat to point to the right and + j hat to point upward. With this convention, the tension vector T points in the + i hat direction, the normal force vector N points in the + j hat direction, and the weight vector w _1 points in the - j hat direction. ---

The Math.

For the connected-block system, Newton’s second law must be applied separately to each block because the tension is an internal interaction between the two blocks.

The forces acting on block m sub 1 are the tension, the normal force, and its weight. The vertical forces on m sub 1 balance, while the horizontal tension provides its acceleration to the right. Applying Newton’s second law to block m sub 1 gives

 
 sum F sub 1x equals m sub 1 a, 
T equals m sub 1 a.
 

The forces acting on block m sub 2 are the upward tension and the downward weight. Since + j hat points upward but m sub 2 accelerates downward, its vertical acceleration is a sub 2y equals -a. Applying Newton’s second law to block m sub 2 gives

 
 sum F sub 2y equals m sub 2 a sub 2y, 
T - m sub 2 g equals - m sub 2 a.
 

These two equations form the governing equations for the system:

 
T equals m sub 1 a, 
T - m sub 2 g equals - m sub 2 a.
 

We solve these equations for the acceleration by substituting T equals m sub 1a into the equation for block m sub 2:

 
 m sub 1a - m sub 2g equals - m sub 2a, 
 m sub 1a + m sub 2a equals m sub 2g, 
( m sub 1 + m sub 2 )a equals m sub 2g, 
a equals m sub 2 over m sub 1 + m sub 2 g.
 

We then use the block m sub 1 equation to solve for the tension:

 
T equals m sub 1a, 
T equals m sub 1 ( m sub 2 over m sub 1 + m sub 2 g ), 
T equals m sub 1m _2 over m sub 1 + m sub 2 g.
 

---

The Conclusion.

The two blocks accelerate with magnitude a equals m sub 2 over m sub 1 + m sub 2 g. Block m sub 1 accelerates to the right, and block m sub 2 accelerates downward. The tension in the string is T equals m sub 1m _2 over m sub 1 + m sub 2 g. The acceleration is less than g because the weight of the hanging block must accelerate both masses, not just m sub 2.

The Model.

Each mass is modeled as a particle connected by a light, inextensible string over a frictionless, massless pulley. The ideal string and pulley make the tension magnitude the same on both sides of the pulley. The string constraint makes the blocks have the same acceleration magnitude.

We choose + j hat to point upward for both blocks. With this convention, the tension on block m sub 1 points in the + j hat direction and the weight of block m sub 1 points in the - j hat direction. The tension on block m sub 2 also points in the + j hat direction, and the weight of block m sub 2 points in the - j hat direction. Since m sub 2 > m sub 1, block m sub 2 accelerates downward and block m sub 1 accelerates upward.

This problem is closely related to the previous attached-blocks example. In both problems, Newton’s second law is applied separately to each block, and the string constraint connects their accelerations.

---

The Math.

For the Atwood machine, Newton’s second law must be applied separately to each block because the tension is an internal interaction between the two masses.

The forces acting on block m sub 1 are the upward tension and the downward weight. Since block m sub 1 accelerates upward in the + j hat direction, Newton’s second law gives

 
 sum F sub 1y equals m sub 1 a, 
T - m sub 1g equals m sub 1a.
 

The forces acting on block m sub 2 are also the upward tension and the downward weight. Since block m sub 2 accelerates downward while + j hat points upward, its acceleration is -a j hat. Newton’s second law gives

 
 sum F sub 2y equals m sub 2 (-a), 
T - m sub 2g equals - m sub 2a.
 

These two equations form the governing equations for the system and will be reused in both parts:

 
T - m sub 1g equals m sub 1a, 
T - m sub 2g equals - m sub 2a.
 

**(a)** First, we determine the acceleration of the blocks after m sub 2 is released. We eliminate the tension by subtracting the equation for block m sub 1 from the equation for block m sub 2 and then solve for a:

 
(T - m sub 2g ) - (T - m sub 1g ) equals - m sub 2a - m sub 1a, 
( m sub 1 - m sub 2 )g equals -( m sub 1 + m sub 2 )a, 
( m sub 2 - m sub 1 )g equals ( m sub 1 + m sub 2 )a, 
a equals m sub 2 - m sub 1 over m sub 1 + m sub 2 g.
 

The acceleration follows from the mass difference and the total mass being accelerated:

 
a equals 4.00 kg - 2.00 kg over 4.00 kg + 2.00 kg (9.81 meters per second squared ) 
 equals 3.27 meters per second squared.
 

**(b)** Next, we find the tension in the string by reusing the equation for block m sub 1. We solve that equation for T and substitute the acceleration found in part **(a)**:

 
T - m sub 1g equals m sub 1a, 
T equals m sub 1 (g+a), 
T equals (2.00 kg )(9.81 meters per second squared + 3.27 meters per second squared ) 
 equals 26.2 newtons.
 

---

The Conclusion.

The blocks accelerate with magnitude a equals 3.27 meters per second squared. Block m sub 2 accelerates downward, and block m sub 1 accelerates upward. The tension in the string is T equals 26.2 newtons. This result closely mirrors the attached-blocks problem: the acceleration is determined by the net unbalanced weight divided by the total mass being accelerated.

 **The Problem**
A 10 -kg mortar shell is fired vertically upward from the ground, with an initial velocity of 50 meters per second as shown in Figure. Determine the maximum height it will travel if atmospheric resistance is measured as F sub D equals (0.01v squared) N, where v is the speed at any instant.

Figure referenced in the course notes.

Expand the dropdown below to see the worked solution.

 
 
 
 
 

The Model.

The mortar shell is modeled as a particle moving vertically. We choose + j hat to point upward and - j hat to point downward. During the upward part of the motion, the velocity points in the + j hat direction, so the drag force points downward in the - j hat direction. The weight also points downward in the - j hat direction. The drag magnitude depends on the instantaneous speed according to F sub D equals kv squared, where k is the drag coefficient in this model. The shell reaches its maximum height when its upward speed becomes zero. The motion is one-dimensional, and g is treated as constant.

---

The Math.

For the upward part of the motion, Newton’s second law relates the downward forces to the vertical acceleration of the shell.

The forces acting on the shell are the weight vector w equals -mg j hat and the drag force vector F _D equals -kv squared j hat. Applying Newton’s second law in the vertical direction gives

 
 sum F sub y equals m a sub y, 
-mg - kv squared equals m a sub y.
 

We solve this equation for the acceleration as a function of speed:

 
 a sub y equals -g - k over m v squared.
 

The acceleration depends on speed, so constant-acceleration kinematics cannot be used. To connect acceleration to position, we use the chain-rule identity

 
 a sub y equals dv over dt 
 equals dv over dy dy over dt 
 equals v dv over dy.
 

We substitute a sub y equals v dv/dy into Newton’s second-law result and separate the variables:

 
v dv over dy equals -g - k over m v squared, 
dy equals - v dv over g+ k over m v squared.
 

The shell starts at y equals 0 with speed v sub 0 and reaches maximum height h when v equals 0. Integrating between these limits gives

 
 integral _0 to the power of h dy equals - integral sub v sub 0 to the power of 0 v dv over g+ k over m v squared,

6.2 Friction

Friction.

Real objects interact with their surroundings, which means the environment can cause a resistance (a force of **friction**). 

Bullet point. Friction opposed relative motion between systems in contact, but also allows us to move. 

Bullet point. Try walking on ice. You need boots that grip (i.e., increase friction).

Friction is one of the common contact forces introduced in Chapter 5. In this chapter, we use friction quantitatively by adding it to the free-body diagram and applying Newton's second law.

Static and Kinetic Friction.

Friction.

 

Friction is a force that opposes relative motion between systems in contact.
 

There are several forms of friction.

Bullet point. Sliding friction f sub s is parallel to the contact surface between systems and is always in a direction that opposes motion (or attempted motion of the systems relative to each other).

Bullet point. Kinetic friction f sub k is when two systems are in contact and moving relative to one another.

For example, friction slows a hockey puck sliding on ice. 

When the static friction is greater than the kinetic friction ( f sub s > f sub k ), the objects are stationary.

Static and Kinetic Friction.

 

If two systems are in contact and stationary relative to one another, then the friction between them is called **static friction.** If two systems are in contact and moving relative to one another, then the friction between them is called **kinetic friction.**

Checkpoint.

 

If an object does not move when pushed, does that mean friction is zero or that static friction has adjusted to match the push?
 

Consider trying to slide a heavy crate across a concrete floor. You might push very hard and not move the crate at all. This means the static friction responds to what you do; it increases (in the opposite direction) to resist your push.

Once in motion, it is easier to keep it in motion than it was to get it started, which indicates that the kinetic friction is less than the static friction. If you add mass to the crate, you need to push even harder to get it started and also to keep it moving. If you oiled the concrete you would find it easier to get the crate started and keep it going.

Figure referenced in the course notes.

A considerable force can be resisted by friction with no apparent motion. The harder the surfaces are pushed together, the more force is needed to move them. Part of the friction is due to adhesive forces between surface molecules of the two objects, which explains the dependence on the surface composition. 

Adhesion varies with substances in contact and is a complicated aspect of surface physics. Once an object is moving, there are fewer points of contact (i.e., fewer molecules adhering), so less force is required to keep the object moving. At small but nonzero speeds, friction is nearly independent of speed.

6.3 Centripetal Force

Centripetal Force.

Chapter 4 introduced centripetal acceleration as the inward acceleration required for circular motion. In this section, we apply Newton's second law to identify which real force, or combination of forces, provides that inward acceleration.

Recall that an object undergoing circular motion must be accelerating because the direction of the object's velocity is changing. This centripetal acceleration is given by:

 vector a _c equals v squared over r r hat, 

where the magnitude of vec v is v (or its speed) and r is the radius of the curve. The velocity vec v is directed along a tangent line to the curve at any instant, which is represented by the unit vector theta hat and is perpendicular to the radial unit vector r hat. 

Note.

The velocity can be represented as vec v equals v theta hat, where v squared is determined through the dot product or 

 vec v dot vec v equals v theta hat dot v theta hat equals v squared 

 

If we also know the angular speed omega (measured in rad/s ), then we can write the magnitude of the centripetal acceleration a sub c in two ways:

 
 a sub c equals v squared over r, 
 equals r omega to the power of 2,
 

where this also referred to as a radial acceleration.

An acceleration must be produced by a force, where any force (or combination of forces) can cause a centripetal acceleration. For example,

Bullet point. the rope on a tether ball,

Bullet point. Earth's gravity on the Moon,

Bullet point. friction between roller skates and the rink floor,

Bullet point. a banked roadway's force on a car, and

Bullet point. forces on the tube of a spinning centrifuge.

Any net force causing uniform circular motion is called a **centripetal force**. The direction of the centripetal force is radial, meaning it is directed in the r hat that is aligned with the circle's radius. The word `centripetal` means 'center-seeking', so the direction is toward the *center of curvature*. 
According to Newton's 2nd law, the net force is mass times acceleration or F_ net equals m a. For uniform circular motion, the acceleration is the centripetal acceleration and the magnitude of the centripetal force is 

 F sub c equals m a sub c. 

Because we had 2 expressions for the centripetal acceleration, we therefore have two expressions for the centripetal force:

 
 F sub c equals mv squared over r, 
 equals mr omega to the power of 2.

6.4 Drag Force and Terminal Speed

Drag Force and Terminal Speed.

You feel the drag force when you move your hand through water or during a strong wind (i.e., outside the car window). 

Bullet point. The faster you move your hand, the harder it is to move. 

Bullet point. You feel a smaller drag force when you tilt your hand so only the side goes through the air (i.e., decreased your surface area in the direction of motion). 

Drag Forces.

The **drag force** always opposes the motion of an object (like friction). Unlike simple friction, the drag force is proportional to *some function* of the velocity of the object in that fluid. Which function to choose is complicated and depends on several factors:

Item 1. shape of the object (its aerodynamics),

Item 2. size,

Item 3. velocity, and

Item 4. the fluid it is in.

For most large objects (e.g., cyclists, cars, baseballs) not moving too slowly, the magnitude of the drag force F_ D is proportional to the *square of the speed* of the object (i.e., F_ D is proportional to v squared ). The full relationship becomes

 
 
 vector F _ D equals 1 over 2 C rho Av squared hat v,
 

where C is the **drag coefficient**, A is the cross-sectional area (i.e., side facing the fluid), and rho is the density of the fluid. *Recall that density is mass per unit volume ( rho equals m/ V ).* 

Bullet point. The more generalized form is F_ D equals bv to the power of n, where the constant b equals 0.5C rho A bundles all the constants together. 

Bullet point. The exponent n equals 2 comes from our assumption about moving at high speed. If the object is moving a low speed then n equals 1.

The value of the drag coefficient is determined empirically either by using a wind tunnel or through a complex numerical simulation on a supercomputer.

At highway speeds, over 50 percent of the power of the car is used to overcome air drag. For average conditions (e.g., altitude and temperature), the mos fuel-efficient cruising speed is about 45 - 50 mph (or about 70 - 80 kilometers per hour ).

Substantial research is undertaken to minimize drag in racing sports as well as specific sporting instruments. Most elite swimmers and cyclists shave their body hair to reduce drag and can slice milliseconds of a race time, which can be the difference between a gold or silver medal.

Terminal Velocity.

Consider a skydiver falling under the influence of gravity. The 2 forces acting on him are the force of gravity F sub g and the drag force F_ D (ignoring the small buoyant force caused by displacing the air). The downward force of gravity remains roughly constant (i.e., the altitude above Earth's surface is much less than Earth's radius) and does not depend on his velocity.

However, the magnitude of the drag force increases with velocity squared (assuming his descent is sufficiently fast) until it equals the gravitational force, thus producing a *net force of zero* and stops accelerating. At this point the person's velocity remains constant and he has reached his **terminal velocity** v_ T.

At the terminal velocity,

 
F_ net equals F_ D - F sub g equals ma equals 0, 
F_ D equals F sub g equals mg.
 

Using Equation, we solve for v_ T 

 
 1 over 2 C rho A v_ T to the power of 2 equals mg, 
v_ T to the power of 2 equals 2mg over rho CA, 
v_ T equals square root of 2mg over rho CA 
 

Checkpoint.

 

At terminal velocity, are the forces gone, or are the upward and downward forces balanced?
 

Assume the density of air is rho equals 1.21 kilograms per cubic meter, and a skydiver with a mass of 75 kg whose cross-sectional area is A equals 0.18 square meters. He has a drag coefficient C equals 0.70, which means we can calculate his terminal velocity v_ T as,

 v_ T equals square root of 2 (75 kg ) (9.81 meters per second squared ) over (1.21 kilograms per cubic meter )(0.70) (0.18 square meters ) equals 98 meters per second equals 350 kilometers per hour. 

This means a skydiver with a with a mass of 75 kg achieves a terminal velocity of

Bullet point. 350 kilometers per hour in a headfirst position (minimizing the area and his drag),

Bullet point. 200 kilometers per hour in a spread-eagle position (area has increased).

Equation does not hold if the object is very small, is going very slow, or is in a denser medium than air. For these cases, we find the drag force is proportional to just the velocity (i.e., F_ D is proportional to v ), This relation is given by **Stokes' law**.

**Stokes' Law**.

 

For a spherical object falling in a medium, the drag force is

 
 vector F _s equals 6 pi r eta vec vv,
 

where r is the radius of the sphere, eta is the viscosity of the fluid, and vec v is the object's velocity.
 

Good examples of Stokes' law are provided by microorganisms, pollen, and dust particles. Each of these objects is small, where we find that many of these objects travel unaided only at a constant (terminal) velocity.

**Example Problem**: Terminal Velocity of a Skydiver.

The Problem.
Find the terminal velocity of an 85 kg skydiver falling in a spread-eagle position. Assume that in the spread-eagle position, the diver has a cross-sectional area of 0.70 square meters.

The Model.

The skydiver is modeled as a particle falling vertically through air. We choose + j hat upward, so the drag force points in the + j hat direction during the downward fall and the weight points in the - j hat direction. At terminal velocity, the skydiver moves at constant speed, so the acceleration is zero. The drag force is modeled with the quadratic drag law. Air density and drag coefficient are treated as constant.

---

The Math.

At terminal velocity, the skydiver requires zero net force because the acceleration is zero.

The forces acting on the skydiver are the upward drag force and the downward weight. Newton’s second law in the vertical direction gives

 
 sum F sub y equals 0, 
 F sub D - mg equals 0, 
 F sub D equals mg.
 

The quadratic drag law gives the magnitude of the drag force:

 
 F sub D equals 1 over 2 rho CA v sub T to the power of 2.

 1 over 2 rho CA v sub T to the power of 2 equals mg,

 v sub T equals square root of 2(85 kg )(9.81 meters per second squared ) over (1.21 kilograms per cubic meter )(1.0)(0.70 square meters )

The terminal velocity of the skydiver in a spread-eagle position is 44 meters per second downward. At this speed, the upward drag force balances the skydiver’s weight, so the acceleration is zero and the speed remains constant.
