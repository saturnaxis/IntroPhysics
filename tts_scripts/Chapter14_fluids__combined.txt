14.1 Fluids, Density, and Pressure

Fluid Mechanics.

Fluids, Density, and Pressure.

Characteristics of Solids and Fluids.

**Solids** are rigid, have specific shapes, and occupy definite volumes. The atoms (or molecules) in a solid are in close proximity to each other, where there is a significant force between the molecules. Solids will take a form that is determined by the forces between the molecules. True solids are compressible, where it usually takes a large force to change the shape of a solid.

Bullet point. The force between the molecules can allow them to organize into a lattice. 

Bullet point. The structure of the 3D lattice is represented as molecules connected by rigid bonds (modeled as stiff springs), which allow *limited freedom of movement.* 

Bullet point. Even a large force produces only small displacements in the atoms (or molecules) of the lattice, and the solid maintains its shape. Solids also resist shearing forces (see Section 12.3).

Liquids and gases are both described as **fluids** of varying viscosity because they yield to shearing forces, whereas solids resist them. Like solids, the molecules in a liquid are bonded to neighboring molecules, but there are fewer (and weaker) bonds. 

Bullet point. The molecules in a liquid are not locked in place and *can move with respect to each other.*

Bullet point. The distance between molecules is similar to the distances in a solid. Liquids have definite volumes, but the shape of a liquid can change depending on the shape of its container.

Bullet point. Gases are **not** bonded to neighboring atoms and *can have large separations* between molecules.

Bullet point. Gases have neither specific shapes nor definite volumes, where their molecules move to fill the container.

Liquids deform easily when stressed and do **not** spring back to their original shape once a force is removed. This occurs because the atoms (or molecules) are free to slide about and change neighbors. Liquids flow with the molecules held together by looser mutual attraction. When a liquid is placed in a container, it remains in the container (given the appropriate temperature and atmospheric pressure). Because the atoms are closely packed liquids resist compression, where an extremely large force is necessary to change the volume of a liquid.

Atoms in gases are separated by large distances and the forces between atoms in a gas are very weak, except when the atoms collide with one another. This makes gases relatively easy to compress and allows them to flow like a fluid. When placed in an open container, gases will escape.

Given the right conditions gases and fluids behave in similar ways, where it is common to drop the distinction and consider them both as fluids. There exists another phase of matter called a **plasma**, which exists at high temperatures, which is characterized by being composed of ions rather than neutral atoms. Plasma has different properties, which requires a detailed description of electrical charges and forces.

Density.

Suppose a block of brass and a block of wood have exactly the same mass. If both blocks are dropped in a tank of water, *why does the wood float* and the brass sink? This occurs because the brass has a greater density than water.

**Density** is an important characteristic of substances because it is crucial in determining whether an object sinks or floats in a fluid.

Pressure.

You are likely familiar with the different kinds of pressure, whether it is your blood pressure or in relation to the weather. But we provide a formal definition of pressure in terms of a force.

Pressure.

 

**{Pressure}** ( P ) is defined as the *normal* force perpendicular force per unit area A over which the force is applied, or

 
P equals perpendicular force over A.
 

To define the pressure at a specific point, the pressure is defined as the force dF exerted by a fluid over an infinitesimal element of area dA containing the point, resulting in p equals dF over dA. 
 

A given force can have a significantly different effect depending on the area over which the force is exerted. For example, a given force F applied to over an area of 1 square millimeters is 100 times as great as the same force applied to an area of 1 square centimeters. That is why a sharp needle is able to poke through the skin with a small force, but the same force does not allow your finger to poke through the skin (see Figure ).

 1 pascals equals 1 newtons per square meter.

14.2 Measuring Pressure

Measuring Pressure.

Gauge vs. Absolute Pressure.

Suppose the pressure gauge on a full scuba tank reads 3000 p s i, which is approximately 207 atm. When the valve is opened, air begins to escape because the pressure inside the tank is grater than the atmospheric pressure outside the tank. Air continues to escape from the tank until the pressure inside the tank equals the pressure of the atmosphere outside the tank. At this point, the pressure gauge on the tank reads, zero even though the pressure inside the tank is actually 1 atm (i.e., the same air pressure outside the tank).

Most pressure gauges are calibrated to read zero at atmospheric pressure. Pressure reading from such gauges are called **gauge pressure**, which is the pressure relative to the atmospheric pressure. When the pressure inside the tank is greater than atmospheric pressure, the gauge reports a positive value.

Some gauges are designed to measure *negative* pressure. For example, many physics experiments must take place in a vacuum chamber where nearly all the air is pumped out. the pressure inside the vacuum chamber is less than atmospheric pressure, so te pressure on the gauge reads a negative value.

Unlike gauge pressure, **absolute pressure** accounts for atmospheric pressure, which in effect adds to the pressure in any fluid not enclosed in a rigid container.

Absolute Pressure.

 

The absolute pressure, or total pressure, is the sum of gauge pressure and atmospheric pressure:

 P_ abs equals P_ g + P_ atm, 

where P_ abs is the absolute pressure, P_ g is the gauge pressure, and P_ atm is the atmospheric pressure.
 

For example, if a tire gauge reads 34 p s i, then the absolute pressure include the atmospheric pressure ( 14.7 p s i ), or 34 p s i + 14.7 p s i equals 48.7 p s i.

In most cases, the absolute pressure in fluids cannot be negative. Fluids push rather than pull, so the smallest absolute pressure in a fluid is zero. Thus, the smallest possible gauge pressure is P_ g equals -P_ atm, which makes P_ abs equals 0. There is no theoretical limit for how large a gauge pressure can be.

A host of devices are used for measuring pressure, ranging from tire pressure gauges to blood pressure monitors. Any property that changes with pressure in a known way can be used to construct a pressure gauge. Some of the most common types include

Bullet point. capacitance pressure gauges, which use the change in electric capacitance due to shape change with pressure,

Bullet point. piezoelectric pressure gauges, which generate a voltage difference across a piezoelectric material under a pressure difference between two sides, and
ion gauges, which measure pressure by ionizing molecules in highly evacuated chambers.

Barometers.

A *barometer* is a device that typically uses a single column of mercury to measure atmospheric pressure, which is in contrast to the U-shaped tube of a manometer. The barometer (invented by Evangelista Torricelli) is constructed from a glass tube closed at one end and filled with mercury. The tube is then inverted and placed in a pool of mercury. A barometer measures atmospheric pressure (rather than gauge pressure) because there is a nearly pure vacuum above the mercury in the tube. The height of the mercury is such that P_ atm equals rho gh and the height changes when the atmospheric pressure varies.

Weather forecasters closely monitor changes in atmospheric pressure because it signals a change in the weather. The barometer can also be used as an altimeter, since the average atmospheric pressure varies with altitude. Mercury barometers and manometer are common enough so that millimeter heights of mercury ( millimeters of mercury ) are quoted for atmospheric and blood pressures.

The Model.

Because both ends of the tube are open, each free surface is exposed to the same atmospheric pressure P naught. In hydrostatic equilibrium, two points at the same height within the same connected liquid must have the same pressure. We therefore compare the pressure at the interface on the right side to the pressure at a point in Liquid 1 on the left side at that same height. The pressure at each point is the atmospheric pressure plus the weight of the fluid column above it.

The Math.

On the left side, the point at the interface level lies beneath a column of Liquid 1 of height h sub 1, so its pressure is

 
P_ left equals P naught + rho _1 g h sub 1.
 

On the right side, the interface lies beneath a column of Liquid 2 of height h sub 2, so its pressure is

 
P_ right equals P naught + rho _2 g h sub 2.
 

Because these two points are at the same height in the same connected fluid, their pressures must be equal. Thus,

 
 P naught + rho _1 g h sub 1 equals P naught + rho _2 g h sub 2.
 

Subtracting P naught from both sides and dividing by g gives

 
 rho _1 h sub 1 equals rho _2 h sub 2.
 

Solving for h sub 1 gives

 
 h sub 1 equals rho _2 over rho _1 h sub 2.
 

The height difference between the two sides is therefore

 
 h sub 2 - h sub 1 equals h sub 2 - rho _2 over rho _1 h sub 2 
 equals (1 - rho _2 over rho _1 ) h sub 2.
 

The Conclusion.

The height difference between the two fluid levels is

 
 h sub 2 - h sub 1 equals (1 - rho _2 over rho _1 ) h sub 2. 
 

This result is consistent with the special case rho _2 equals rho _1, for which the two heights become equal.

14.3 Pascal's Principle and Hydraulics

Pascal's Principle and Hydraulics.

Pascal's Principle.

In 1653, Blaise Pascal published his *Treatise on the Equilibrium of Liquids*, in which he develops his principles of static fluids. When a fluid is not flowing (i.e., static), we say that the fluid is in static equilibrium. If the fluid is water, we say it is in hydrostatic equilibrium. For a fluid in static equilibrium, the net force on any part of the fluid must be zero because the fluid will would otherwise start to flow.

Pascal's observations provide the foundation for hydraulics. Pascal observed that a *change in pressure* applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished throughout the fluid and to the walls of the container. **Pascal's principle** implies that the total pressure in a fluid is the sum of the pressures from different sources. 

Note that this principle does not say that the pressure is the *same* at all points of a fluid. A good example is the fluid at a depth depends on the depth of the fluid and the pressure of the atmosphere. Rather, Pascal's principle applies to the *change* in pressure.

Figure referenced in the course notes.

Suppose you place some water in a cylindrical container of height h and cross-sectional area A that has a movable piston of mass m (see Figure ). Adding a weight w equals Mg at the top of the piston increases the pressure at the top by w/A equals Mg/A, since the additional weight also acts over the area A of the lid by

 change in p_ top equals Mg over A. 

According to Pascal's principle, the pressure at all points in the water changes by the same amount, Mg/A. Thus the pressure at the bottom also increases by Mg/A. The pressure at the bottom of the container is equal to the sum of 

Bullet point. the atmospheric pressure, 

Bullet point. the pressure due to the fluid, and 

Bullet point. the pressure supplied by the mass.

The change in pressure at the bottom of the container due to the mass is 

 change in p_ bottom equals Mg over A. 

Since the pressure changes are the same everywhere in the fluid, we no longer need subscripts to designate the pressure change for the top or bottom, where

 change in p equals change in p_ top equals change in p_ bottom equals change in p_ everywhere.

Applications of Pascal's Principle and Hydraulic Systems.

Hydraulic systems are used to operate automotive brakes, hydraulic jacks, and many other mechanical systems (see Figure ). We can derive a relationship between the forces in a simple hydraulic system by applying Pascal's principle.

The two pistons (in the figure) are at the same height, so there is no difference in pressure due to a difference in depth. The pressure due to F sub 1 action on area A sub 1 is simply

 P sub 1 equals F sub 1 over A sub 1. 

According to Pascal's principle, the pressure is transmitted undiminished throughout the fluid and to all walls of the container. Thus, a pressure P sub 2 is felt at the other piston that is equal to P sub 1 (i.e., P sub 1 equals P sub 2 ). However, P sub 2 equals F sub 2 / A sub 2, for this to be so, we find that

 
 F sub 1 over A sub 1 equals F sub 2 over A sub 2.
 

This equation relates the ratios of force to area in any hydraulic system, provided that the pistons are at the same vertical height and that friction in the system is negligible.

Hydraulic systems can increase or decrease the force applied to them. To mak the force larger, te pressure is applied to a larger area. Hydraulic systems are analogous to simple levers, but they have the advantage tha pressure can be sent through curved lines to several places at once.

The **hydraulic jack** is used to lift heavy loads, such as those used by auto mechanics to raise an automobile. It consists of an incompressible fluid filled in a U-tube fitted with a movable piston on each side. One side of the U-tube is narrower than the other. A small force applied over a small area can balance a much larger force on the other side over a larger area (see Figure ).

 F sub 1 equals ( A sub 1 over A sub 2 ) F sub 2,

The Problem.
Consider the automobile hydraulic system shown in. Suppose a force of 100 newtons is applied to the brake pedal, which acts on the pedal cylinder (acting as a “master” cylinder) through a lever. A force of 500 newtons is exerted on the pedal cylinder. Pressure created in the pedal cylinder is transmitted to the four wheel cylinders. The pedal cylinder has a diameter of 0.500 cm and each wheel cylinder has a diameter of 2.50 cm. Calculate the magnitude of the force F sub 2 created at each of the wheel cylinders.

Figure referenced in the course notes.

The Model.

We treat the brake fluid as an ideal fluid in static equilibrium, so Pascal’s principle applies. The pressure is transmitted equally throughout the fluid, which means the pressure in the master cylinder equals the pressure in each wheel cylinder. If F sub 1 is the force applied to the pedal cylinder, and A sub 1 and A sub 2 are the cross-sectional areas of the pedal and wheel cylinders, respectively, then F sub 1 / A sub 1 equals F sub 2 / A sub 2. Because the given 500 newtons is already the force exerted on the pedal cylinder, the lever analysis is not needed here.

---

The Math.

We begin with Pascal’s principle, which states that the pressure is the same in both cylinders. This gives the relationship F sub 1 / A sub 1 equals F sub 2 / A sub 2, where F sub 1 is the force applied to the pedal cylinder and F sub 2 is the force exerted by each wheel cylinder. Solving this equation for the output force gives F sub 2 equals ( A sub 2 / A sub 1 ) F sub 1.

Because both cylinders have circular cross sections, their areas can be written in terms of their radii as A equals pi r squared. Substituting this into the area ratio shows that the factor pi cancels, leaving A sub 2 / A sub 1 equals r sub 2 to the power of 2 / r sub 1 to the power of 2. This allows us to express the force entirely in terms of the cylinder radii.

The given diameters of the pedal and wheel cylinders are 0.500 cm and 2.50 cm, respectively, so the corresponding radii are r sub 1 equals 0.250 cm and r sub 2 equals 1.25 cm. Substituting these values into the force relation gives

 
 F sub 2 equals ( r sub 2 to the power of 2 over r sub 1 to the power of 2 ) F sub 1 
 equals ( (1.25 cm ) squared over (0.250 cm ) squared )(500 newtons ) 
 equals 25(500 newtons ) 
 equals 1.25 times ten to the 4 newtons.
 

---

The Conclusion.

Each wheel cylinder exerts a force of 1.25 times ten to the 4 newtons. This result is significantly larger than the 500 newtons force applied to the pedal cylinder, which reflects the large ratio of the cylinder areas in the hydraulic system. Because pressure is transmitted equally throughout the fluid, increasing the cross-sectional area of the output cylinder increases the force it exerts.

This amplification of force explains how a driver can apply a relatively small force at the pedal and still generate a much larger braking force at the wheels. However, this increase in force comes at the expense of distance—the wheel cylinders move a smaller distance than the pedal cylinder, so the total work done remains consistent with energy conservation.

*Pressure increases with depth in a fluid!*

This means that the upward force on the bottom of an object (in a fluid) is greater than the downward force on top of the object. There is an upward, or **buoyant**, force on any object in any fluid (see Figure ).

Figure referenced in the course notes.

Bullet point. If the buoyant force is greater than the object's weight, the net force on the object is upward. If we release such an object, it will rise in the fluid. 

Bullet point. If the buoyant force is less than the object's weight, the object sinks when released. 

Bullet point. If the buoyant force equals the object's weight, the object can remain suspended at its present depth, either immersed in the fluid or partly above the surface.

The buoyant force is always present, whether the object floats, sinks, or is suspended in a fluid.

Buoyant Force.

 

The buoyant force is the upward force on any object in any fluid.
 

Archimedes' Principle.

*Just how large is the buoyant force?*

Consider what happens when a submerged object is removed from a fluid (see Figure ). If the object were not in the fluid, the space the object occupied would be filled by fluid having a weight w_ fl. This weight is supported by the surrounding fluid, so the buoyant force must equal w_ fl, or the weight of the fluid displaced by the object.

Figure referenced in the course notes.

Archimedes' Principle.

 

The buoyant force on an object equals the weight of the fluid it displaces. In equation form, **Archimedes' principle** is 

 F_ B equals w_ fl, 

where F_ B is the buoyant force and w_ fl is the weight of the fluid displaced by the object.

This principle is named after the Greek mathematician and inventor Archimedes of Syracuse, who state this principle long before concepts of force were well established.
 

Archimedes' principle refers to the force of buoyancy that results when a body is submerged in a fluid, whether partially or wholly. The force that provides the pressure of a fluid acts on a body perpendicular to the surface. The force due to the pressure at the bottom is pointed up, while at the tope, the force due to the pressure is pointed down. The forces due to pressures at the sides are pointing *into* the body.

Since the bottom of the body is at a greater depth than the top, the pressure at the lower part of the body is higher than the pressure at the upper part (as shown in the figure). Therefore a net upward force acts on the body. This upward force is the force of buoyancy.

Density and Archimedes' Principle.

If you drop a lump of clay in water, it will sink. But if you mold the same lump of clay into the shape of a boat, then it will float. The clay boat *displaces* more water than the lump and experiences a greater buoyant force, even though its mass is the same.

The average density of an object is what ultimately determines whether it floats. If an object's average density is less than that of the surrounding fluid, it will float. The fluid has a higher density, which means contains more mass for a given volume and hence more weight. The buoyant force is greater than the weight of the object. An object denser than the fluid will sink.

In Figure, the unloaded ship has a lower density and less of it is submerged compared with the loaded ship. We can derive a quantitative expression for the fraction submerged by considering density. 

Figure referenced in the course notes.

The fraction submerged is the ratio of the volume submerged to the volume of the object, or

 fraction submerged equals V_ sub over V_ obj equals V_ fl over V_ obj. 

The volume submerged V_ sub equals the volume of the fluid displaced V_ fl. We can obtain the relationship between the densities by substituting rho equals m/V, or V equals m/ rho. This gives

 V_ fl over V_ obj equals ( m_ fl over rho _ fl ) ( rho _ obj over m_ obj ) equals ( m_ fl over m_ obj ) ( rho _ obj over rho _ fl ), 

where rho _ obj is the average density of the object and rho _ fl is the density of the fluid. Since the object floats, its mass and that of the fluid displaced are equal (i.e., m_ obj equals m_ fl ), leaving

 V_ fl over V_ obj equals rho _ obj over rho _ fl. 

We can determine the density of an unknown fluid using an object of a known density and measuring the fraction of it that is submerged.

14.6 Bernoulli's Equation

Bernoulli's Equation.

When a fluid flows into a narrower channel, its speed increases which means that its kinetic energy increases as well. The increased kinetic energy come from the net work don on the fluid to push it into the channel. If the fluid changes vertical position, work is also done on the fluid by the gravitational force.

A pressure difference occurs when the channel narrows, where this difference results in a net force on the fluid because the P times A equals F, and this net force does work. Recall the work-energy theorem,

 W_ net equals change in K equals K - K sub o equals 1 over 2 mv squared - 1 over 2 meters v sub o to the power of 2. 

The net work done increases the fluid's kinetic energy. As a result, the pressure drops in a rapidly moving fluid whether or not the fluid is confined to a tube.

For example, shower curtains have a disagreeable habit of bulging into the shower stall when the shower is on. The high-velocity stream of water and air creates a region of lower pressure inside the shower, whereas the pressure on the other side remains the same (at the standard atmospheric pressure). This pressure difference results in a net force, pushing the curtain inward.

Similarly, when a car passes a semi on the highway, the two vehicles pull toward each other. The reason is the same: The high velocity of the air between the car and the truck creates a region of lower pressure between the vehicles, and they are pushed together by greater pressure on the outside (see Figure ). THis effect was observed as back as the mid-1800s, when it was found that trains passing in opposite directions tipped precariously toward one another.

Figure referenced in the course notes.

Bernoulli's Equation.

 

For an ideal fluid moving steadily, the pressure and energy density is conserved along a flow line. The conservation relation is given by

 
P + 1 over 2 rho v squared + rho gh equals constant,
 

where P is the pressure, rho is the mass density, v is the speed of the fluid, and h is the height.

In a dynamic situation, the pressures at the same height in different parts of the fluid may be different if they have different speeds of flow.

 

Analyzing Bernoulli's Equation.

If we follow a small volume of fluid along its path, the sum of pressure and energy density remains the same even though various individual quantities may change. Bernoulli's equation is just a convenient statement of the conservation of energy for an *incompressible* fluid in the absence of friction. 

Bernoulli's equation for *static* fluids.

Consider the simple solution where the fluid is static (i.e., v sub 1 equals v sub 2 equals 0 ). Bernoulli's equation in that case is

 P sub 1 + rho g h sub 1 equals P sub 2 + rho g h sub 2. 

We can simplify the equation by measuring the relative height h as the difference between h sub 2 and h sub 1, or h equals h sub 2 - h sub 1. In this case, we get

 P sub 2 equals P sub 1 + rho gh. 

This equation tells us tha the pressure increases with depth for static fluids. As we go from point 1 to point 2 in the fluid, the depth increases by h and the difference in pressure is equal to rho gh. Thus, Bernoulli's equation confirms the fact that pressure change due to the weight of a fluid is rho gh. 

Bernoulli's principle.

Suppose a fluid is *moving* but its depth is constant (i.e, h sub 1 equals h sub 2 ). Under this condition, Bernoulli's equation becomes

 P sub 1 + 1 over 2 rho v sub 1 to the power of 2 equals P sub 2 + 1 over 2 rho v sub 2 to the power of 2. 

Situations in which fluid flows at a constant depth are so common that this equation is often called **Bernoulli's principle**, which is simply Bernoulli's equation for a constant depth. Bernoulli's principle reinforces that pressure drops as the speed increases in a moving fluid: If v sub 2 > v sub 1 in the equation, then P sub 2 must be less than P sub 1 for the equality to hold. It is more easily seen by

 P sub 1 - P sub 2 equals 1 over 2 rho ( v sub 2 to the power of 2 - v sub 1 to the power of 2 ), 

because v squared>0 and P sub 1 > P sub 2 must be true for the left-side to be positive.

Viscosity and Turbulence.

_This audio is AI-generated._

Recall (from Chapter 6) that an object sliding across the floor with an initial velocity and no applied force will come to rest due to the frictional force. Friction depends on the types of materials in contact and is proportional to the normal force.

Viscosity and Laminar Flow.

When you pour yourself a glass of juice, the liquid flows freely and quickly. But if you pour syrups or honey, the liquid flows and sticks to the container. Water has a very low viscosity, while honey (or molasses) has a much higher viscosity.

The precise definition of viscosity is based on laminar (i.e., nonturbulent) flow. Figure shows schematically how laminar and turbulent flow differ. When flow is laminary, *layers* flow without mixing. On the other hand, turbulent flow has the layers mixing and significant velocities occur in directions other than the overall direction of flow.

Item 1. Any obstruction or sharp corner (e.g., a faucet) creates turbulent by imparting velocities perpendicular to the flow.

Item 2. The drag between adjacent layers of fluid and between the fluid and its surroundings can form swirls and eddies when the speed is great enough.

Laminar Flow Confined to Tubes: Poiseuille's Law.

*What causes flow?*

It is a pressure difference. There is a very simple relationship between horizontal flow and pressure. Flow rate Q is in the direction from high to low pressure. The greater the pressure differential between two points, the greater the flow rate. This relationship can be stated as

 
 

Q equals P sub 2 - P sub 1 over R,
 

where P sub 1 and P sub 2 are the pressures a the two points and R is the resistance to flow. The resistance R includes everything, except pressure, that affects flow rate. For example R is greater for a long tube than for a short one. The greater the viscosity of a fluid, the greater the value of R. Turbulence greatly increases R, whereas increasing the diameter of a tube decreases R.

If viscosity is zero, the fluid is frictionless and the resistance to flow is also zero. Comparing frictionless flow in a tube to viscous flow (see the figure), we see that for a viscous fluid, speed is greatest at midstream because of drag at the boundaries. We can see the effect of viscosity in a Bunsen burner flame, even though viscosity of natural gas is small.

Figure referenced in the course notes.

The resistance R to laminar flow of an incompressible fluid with viscosity eta through a horizontal tube of uniform radius r and length l is given by

 
R equals 8 eta l over pi r to the power of 4.
 

This equation is called **Poiseuille's law for resistance**, named after J. L Poiseuille who derived it in an attempt to understand the flow of blood through the body.

In the expression for R, we see that resistance is directly proportional to both the fluid viscosity eta and the length l of a tube. Both of these directly affect the amount of friction encountered, where the greater the resistance, the smaller the flow. The radius r of a tube also affects the resistance, which makes sense because a larger radius allows for a greater flow (all other factors remaining the same). 

Note.

In Poiseuille's law, r is raised to the **fourth** power. This exponent means that any change in the radius of a tube has a very large effect on resistance. For example, doubling the radius of a tube decreases resistance by a factor of 2 to the power of 4 equals 16.

 P sub 2 - P sub 1 equals RQ.
 

In this case, P sub 2 is the pressure at the water works and R is the resistance of the water main. During times of heavy use, the flow rate Q is large. This means that P sub 2 - P sub 1 must also be large (i.e., P sub 2 gg P sub 1 ). Therefore, P sub 1 must decrease assuming that P sub 2 stays the same. It is correct to think of flow and resistance as causing the pressure to drop from P sub 2 to P sub 1. *The equation P sub 2 - P sub 1 equals RQ is valid for both laminar and turbulent flows.*

We can also ues this relation to analyze pressure drops occurring in more complex systems in which the tube radius is not the same everywhere Resistance is much greater in narrow places (e.g. an obstructed coronary artery). For a given flow rate Q, the pressure drop is greatest where the tube is most narrow. This is how water faucets control flow.

Additionally, R is greatly increased by turbulence, and a constriction that creates turbulence greatly reduces the pressure downstream. Plaque in an artery reduces pressure and hence flow, both by its resistance and by the turbulence it creates.

Measuring Turbulence.

An indicator called the **Reynolds number** N sub R can reveal whether flow is laminar or turbulent. For flow in a tube of uniform diameter, the Reynolds number is defined as 

 
 N sub R equals 2 over eta rho v r, (flow in tube) 
 

where rho is the fluid density, v its speed, eta its viscosity, and r the tube radius. The Reynolds number is a *dimensionless* quantity. Experiments have revealed that N sub R is related to the onset of turbulence. 

Bullet point. For N sub R below about 2000, the flow is laminar.

Bullet point. For N sub R above 3000, the flow is turbulent.

For 2000 < N sub R < 3000, the flow is unstable. It can be laminar, but small obstructions and surface roughness can make it turbulent. It may also oscillate randomly between being laminar and turbulent. In fact, the flow of a fluid with a Reynolds number between 2000-3000# is a good example of chaotic behavior.

Tip.

A system is defined to be chaotic when its behavior is so sensitive to the choice of the initial state, it is extremely difficult to predict precisely any future state.
 

It is difficult (but not impossible) to predict whether the flow is turbulent or not when a fluid's Reynold's number falls in this range due to extremely sensitive dependence on factors like roughness and obstructions on the nature of the flow. A tiny variation in one factor has an exaggerated (or nonlinear) effect on the flow.

**Example Problem**: Air Conditioning Systems.

The Problem.
An air conditioning system is being designed to supply air at a gauge pressure of 0.054 pascals at a temperature of 20 degrees C. The air is sent through an insulated, round conduit with a diameter of 18.00 cm. The conduit is 20 meters long and is open to a room at atmospheric pressure 101.30 kilopascals. The room has a length of 12 meters, a width of 6 meters, and a height of 3 meters. (a) What is the volume flow rate through the pipe, assuming laminar flow? (b) Estimate the length of time to completely replace the air in the room. (c) The builders decide to save money by using a conduit with a diameter of 9.00 cm. What is the new flow rate?

Expand the dropdown below to see the worked solution.

 
 
 
 
 

The Model.

We model the air as a fluid undergoing steady laminar flow through a round conduit, so Poiseuille’s law applies. The conduit is treated as cylindrical with constant radius along its length, and the pressure difference driving the flow is the given gauge pressure relative to the room. Because the room is open to the atmosphere, the outlet pressure is atmospheric, so the gauge pressure directly gives the pressure difference across the conduit. To estimate the air-replacement time, we treat the volume flow rate as constant and divide the room volume by that flow rate. In part (c), the conduit length, pressure difference, and viscosity remain unchanged, so the new flow rate depends only on the smaller radius through the r to the power of 4 dependence of Poiseuille flow.

---

The Math.

**(a)** For steady laminar flow through a cylindrical conduit, Poiseuille’s law gives the volume flow rate in terms of the pressure difference, viscosity, conduit length, and conduit radius. The system requires the volume flow rate, given by

 
Q equals change in P pi r to the power of 4 over 8 eta l.
 

The pressure difference is the given gauge pressure, so change in P equals 0.054 pascals. From Table 14.4, the viscosity of air at 20 degrees C is eta equals 0.0181 times ten to the minus 3 pascals dot s equals 1.81 times ten to the minus 5 pascals dot s. The conduit diameter is 18.00 cm, so the radius is r equals 9.00 cm equals 0.0900 meters, and the conduit length is l equals 20 meters. Substituting these values gives

 
Q equals (0.054 pascals ) pi (0.0900 meters ) to the power of 4 over 8(1.81 times ten to the minus 5 pascals dot s )(20 meters ) 
 equals 3.84 times ten to the minus 3 cubic meters/s.
 

**(b)** To estimate the time required to replace the air in the room, we first determine the room volume. For a rectangular room, the volume is the product of its length, width, and height. Thus,

 
V equals (12 meters )(6 meters )(3 meters ) 
 equals 216 cubic meters.
 

For a constant volume flow rate, the replacement time is the room volume divided by the flow rate. Therefore,

 
 change in t equals V over Q 
 equals 216 cubic meters over 3.84 times ten to the minus 3 cubic meters/s 
 equals 5.63 times ten to the 4 s.
 

Converting this time to hours gives

 
 change in t equals 5.63 times ten to the 4 s over 3600 s/hr 
 equals 15.6 hr.
 

**(c)** For the smaller conduit, the new diameter is 9.00 cm, so the new radius is r' equals 4.50 cm equals 0.0450 meters. Using Poiseuille’s law again with the same pressure difference, viscosity, and conduit length gives

 
Q' equals change in P pi r' to the power of 4 over 8 eta l 
 equals (0.054 pascals ) pi (0.0450 meters ) to the power of 4 over 8(1.81 times ten to the minus 5 pascals dot s )(20 meters ) 
 equals 2.40 times ten to the minus 4 cubic meters/s.
 

This result is also consistent with the radius dependence Q is proportional to r to the power of 4. Halving the radius reduces the flow rate by a factor of 2 to the power of 4 equals 16, so the new flow rate should be one-sixteenth of the original value:

 
Q' equals Q over 16 equals 3.84 times ten to the minus 3 cubic meters/s over 16 equals 2.40 times ten to the minus 4 cubic meters/s.
 

---

The Conclusion.

The volume flow rate through the original conduit is 3.84 times ten to the minus 3 cubic meters/s. At this rate, the time required to replace the 216 cubic meters of air in the room is 15.6 hr. If the conduit diameter is reduced from 18.00 cm to 9.00 cm, the new flow rate becomes 2.40 times ten to the minus 4 cubic meters/s.

These results show that the flow rate is extremely sensitive to the conduit radius in laminar flow. Halving the diameter halves the radius, which reduces the flow rate by a factor of sixteen. This dramatic reduction explains why even a modest decrease in duct size can greatly increase the time required to move air through a system.

 **The Problem**
In Example 14.8, we found the volume flow rate of an air conditioning system to be Q equals 3.84 times ten to the minus 3 cubic meters/s. This calculation assumed laminar flow. (a) Was this a good assumption? (b) At what velocity would the flow become turbulent?

Expand the dropdown below to see the worked solution.

 
 
 
 
 

The Model.

We model the air as flowing steadily through a round conduit of radius 0.0900 meters. To test whether the laminar-flow assumption is valid, we first determine the average fluid speed from the known volume flow rate and conduit cross section. We then use the Reynolds number to classify the flow. For part (b), the onset of turbulence is estimated by taking the Reynolds number to be 2000, which is the usual threshold between laminar and turbulent flow in a pipe.

---

The Math.

**(a)** To determine whether the flow is laminar, we first need the average speed of the air in the conduit. For steady flow through a circular cross section, the volume flow rate is related to the speed by Q equals Av, where A equals pi r squared. Solving for the speed gives

 
v equals Q over pi r squared.
 

The volume flow rate is Q equals 3.84 times ten to the minus 3 cubic meters/s, and the conduit radius is r equals 0.0900 meters. Substituting these values gives

 
v equals 3.84 times ten to the minus 3 cubic meters/s over pi (0.0900 meters ) squared 
 equals 0.151 meters per second.
 

To classify the flow, we use the Reynolds number for flow in a pipe. For a pipe of radius r, the Reynolds number is

 
N_ R equals 2 rho v r over eta.
 

At 20 degrees C, the density of air is rho equals 1.29 kilograms per cubic meter and its viscosity is eta equals 1.81 times ten to the minus 5 pascals dot s. Substituting these values together with v equals 0.151 meters per second and r equals 0.0900 meters gives

 
N_ R equals 2(1.29 kilograms per cubic meter )(0.151 meters per second )(0.0900 meters ) over 1.81 times ten to the minus 5 pascals dot s 
 equals 1940.
 

Because this value is less than 2000, the flow is in the laminar regime.

**(b)** To determine the speed at which the flow becomes turbulent, we set the Reynolds number equal to the threshold value 2000 and solve for v. Starting from N_ R equals 2 rho v r/ eta, dividing both sides by 2 rho r gives

 
v equals N_ R eta over 2 rho r.
 

Substituting N_ R equals 2000, eta equals 1.81 times ten to the minus 5 pascals dot s, rho equals 1.29 kilograms per cubic meter, and r equals 0.0900 meters gives

 
v equals (2000)(1.81 times ten to the minus 5 pascals dot s ) over 2(1.29 kilograms per cubic meter )(0.0900 meters ) 
 equals 0.156 meters per second.
 

---

The Conclusion.

The Reynolds number for the air-conditioning flow is 1940, so the assumption of laminar flow is reasonable. The flow becomes turbulent when the speed reaches about 0.156 meters per second. Because the operating speed of 0.151 meters per second is only slightly below this threshold, the flow is just barely in the laminar regime.
