7.1 Work

Work and Kinetic Energy.

Chapter roadmap.

 

This chapter connects forces, displacement, and changes in motion using the ideas of work and kinetic energy. Work measures the part of a force that transfers energy along a displacement, and kinetic energy measures the energy associated with an object's speed.

As you read, focus on three questions:

Item 1. Which forces do work on the object?

Item 2. Is the work done by each force positive, negative, or zero?

Item 3. How does the net work change the object's kinetic energy?

These ideas will be used to analyze motion with friction, springs, curved paths, and power.
 

Work.

In physics, **work** isn't just about what makes you tired or fatigued. It has a special definition, which is when energy is transferred to an object due to the work done on it. Work is done when a *force* acts on something that undergoes a *displacement* from one position to another.

Forces can vary as a function of position, and displacements can be along various paths between two points. We define the increment of work dW done by a force vector F acting through an infinitesimal displacement d vector r as the dot product of these two vectors:

 
dW equals vector F dot d vector r, 
 equals | vector F ||d vector r | cos{ theta }, 
 equals (F)( change in r) cos{ theta }.
 

We can find the total work done by adding up the contributions for infinitesimal displacements (along a path between two positions). This uses the same dot product idea introduced in Chapter 2.

Work Done by a Force.

 

The work done by a force is the integral of the force with respect to the displacement along the path of the displacement:

 
 
W sub AB equals integral _A to the power of B vector F dot d vector r.
 

The path or line integral adds up contributions along a path. Often the path is parameterized by time so there are time dependent functions x(t) and y(t) that are separable. In short, we add up all the components of the force that are parallel to the direction of the path (as shown in 
the figure)
 

Figure referenced in the course notes.

Although we expressed the dot product using the product of magnitudes and the cosine of the angle between them. If it is convenient, one could use the form of the dot product that includes the vector components.

Note.

Recall that the vector components of force are portions of a force along a given direction. The components can be positive, negative, or zero, depending on whether the force is directed along the path or perpendicular to it. The maximum work is done either parallel ( cos{0} equals 1 ) or antiparallel ( cos{ pi } equals -1 ), while *zero* work is done when the force is perpendicular to the displacement ( cos{ theta } equals 0 ).
 

The units of work are force multiplied by units of length, which in the SI system is N dot m. This combination is called a **joule** and is abbreviated as J. In the English system (used in the US), the unit of work is the foot-pound ( ft dot lb ) because the unit of force is the pound ( lb ) and the displacement is the foot ( ft ).

Work Done by Constant Forces and Contact Forces.

The simplest work to evaluate is that done by a constant magnitude force in a non-varying (single) direction. In this case, the work is only determined by the distance traveled.

Work is an integral. Consider a rectangle of height h and a length ell. If the length is divided into many ( n ) small chunks of length change in el l sub n. You can visualize that the area of the rectangle can be calculated by summing up all smaller areas, i.e., A equals su m sub n h times change in el l sub n equals h times ell.

7.2 Kinetic Energy

Kinetic Energy.

It's plausible to suppose that a larger velocity of a body will have a greater effect on other bodies. This does not depend on the *direction* of the velocity only its magnitude (i.e., speed). The larger effect can be translated into a larger potential to do work once the fast-moving body strikes a slower one.

An example is through billiards (or pool), when the fast-moving (white) cue ball strikes the blue-colored 2 ball. The faster the cue, the more the 2 ball is displaced. The cue ball must have some "energy" that is transferred to the 2 ball. The cue must have some "energy of motion" as it is traveling across the table. During the 18th century (i.e., 1700s), the "energy of motion" was renamed to **kinetic energy**.

**Physics Demos**.

 

 
 

The kinetic energy has two definitions: (1) classical and (2) relativistic. The relativistic definition only pertains to objects moving near the speed of light, which we are not investigating here. We'll stick with the classical definition.

Kinetic Energy.

 

The kinetic energy K of a particle is one-half the product of the particle's mass m and the square of its speed v. We extend this definition to any system of particles by adding up the kinetic energies of all the constituent particles to get

 
 K sub i equals 1 over 2 meters sub iv _i squared, ( single particle i) 
K equals sum sub i equals 1 to the power of N 1 over 2 meters sub iv _i squared. ( system of particles )
 

Recall from Newton's 2nd Law and Momentum, we represented Newton's 2nd law in a different form using the momentum vector p equals m vector v. We can do the same here using the magnitude p equals sqrt{ vector p dot vector p } equals mv, where the kinetic energy becomes

 K equals 1 over 2 meters ( p over m ) squared equals p squared over 2m, 

which is sometimes more convenient to use.

 

The units of kinetic energy are the mass times the speed squared, or kg dot square meters/s squared. Recalling that the units for work are N dot m equals kg dot meters per second squared dot m equals kg dot square meters/s squared, which are joules ( J ). Work and kinetic energy have the same units because they are different forms of the same (more general) physical property.

Because velocity is a relative quantity, the kinetic energy must depend on your frame of reference. A frame of reference is chosen to simplify your calculations and subsequent analysis.

Bullet point. One frame of reference is external to the system (i.e., where the observations are made).

Bullet point. Another frame lies attached to (or moves with) the system as an internal frame.

The equations for relative motion provide a link to calculating the kinetic energy of an object with respect to different frames of reference. For example,

 K equals 1 over 2 meters( v sub A plus or minus v sub B ) squared 

represents the kinetic energy of an object with speed v sub A that is moving relative to another object v sub B. Depending on the relative direction of motion, we can determine whether there is a + or -.

7.3 Work-Energy Theorem

Work-Energy Theorem.

According to Newton's 2nd law, the net force (all forces acting on a particle) determines the rate of change in the momentum of the particle or its motion. Therefore, we should consider the work done by all the forces acting on a particle, or the **net work**, to see what effect it has on the particle's motion.

Consider the net work done on a particle that moves over an infinitesimal displacement, or

 dW_ net equals vector F _ net dot d vector r. 

Newton's 2nd law tells us that we can transform the net force vector F _ net using the acceleration d vec v over dt, so

 dW_ net equals m ( d vec v over dt ) dot d vector r. 

Using the definition of velocity, d vector r equals vec v dt, we have:

 
dW_ net equals m ( d vec v over dt ) dot vec v dt, 
 equals m vec v dot ( d vec v over dt )dt, 
 equals m vec v dot d vec v,
 

where we also used the commutative property of the dot product.

We can represent the dot product in terms of Cartesian coordinates and then include the result in the integral between two points A and B on the particle's trajectory. This gives us the work done on the particle as:

 
W sub net,AB equals integral _A to the power of B m v sub x d v sub x + m v sub y d v sub y + m v sub z d v sub z, 
 equals 1 over 2 meters ( v sub x to the power of 2 + v sub y to the power of 2 + v sub z to the power of 2 ) r evaluated from _A to the power of B, 
 equals 1 over 2 mv squared r evaluated from _A to the power of B equals K sub B - K sub A.
 

We used the relation for v squared equals v sub x to the power of 2 + v sub y to the power of 2 + v sub z to the power of 2 to reduce the expression and the definition of a particle's kinetic energy K. The result is called the **work-energy theorem**#Work percentE2 percent80 percent93energ y sub principle ).

Work-Energy Theorem.

 

The net work on a particle equals the change in the particle's kinetic energy:

 
 
 
W_ net equals K sub f - K sub i 
 equals 1 over 2 meters ( v sub f to the power of 2 - v sub i to the power of 2 ).
 
 
 

According to this theorem, when an object 

Bullet point. slows down ( v sub f v sub i ), its final kinetic energy is greater than the initial and the net work on it is *positive*.

When calculating the net work, you must include **all** the forces acting on an object. If you leave out any forces or include extra forces not acting on the object, you will get a wrong result.

The importance of the work-energy theorem is that it makes some calculations much simpler to accomplish compared to solving them with Newton's 2nd law. For example, we obtained the acceleration of a block on a frictionless incline as a equals g sin{ theta } using Newton's second law in Chapter 5 (**Example Problem**: Weight on an incline). If we also use the kinematic equation for acceleration 

 v sub f to the power of 2 equals v sub i to the power of 2 +2a( s sub f - s sub i ), 

then we could find that 

 v sub f to the power of 2 equals v sub i to the power of 2 + 2g( s sub f - s sub i ) sin{ theta }, 

where s sub f and s sub i are the initial and final positions on the incline, respectively. Once Newton's second law gives the acceleration, the same result follows from the kinematics tools in Chapter 3.

We can also get this result from the work-energy theorem in Equation. Since only two forces are acting on the object (gravity and the normal force; normal force doesn't do work), the net work is just the work done by gravity. The infinitesimal work dW is the dot product of gravity ( vector F _g equals -mg j hat ) and the infinitesimal displacement ( d vector r equals dx i hat + dy j hat ). Applying the work-energy theorem, we have

 
W_ net equals integral sub y sub i to the power of y sub f vector F _g dot d vector r equals integral sub y sub i to the power of y sub f -mg dy 
 equals -mg integral sub y sub i to the power of y sub f dy equals -mgy r evaluated from sub y sub i to the power of y sub f 
 equals -mg ( y sub f - y sub i ). 
 

We have found the left-hand side of the work-energy theorem, where we now set it equal to the change in kinetic energy:

 -mg ( y sub f - y sub i ) equals 1 over 2 meters ( v sub f to the power of 2 - v sub i to the power of 2 ). 

In **Example Problem**: Weight on an incline the change in height ( y sub f - y sub i ) forms the short side of a right triangle with the same angle theta from the incline. The hypotenuse of the triangle is ( s sub f - s sub i ) along the incline. Therefore, we can show that the short side of the triangle is ( y sub f - y sub i ) equals -( s sub f - s sub i ) sin{ theta } using our trigonometric identity for sin{ theta }. *Note: s is defined to increase down the incline (to the left in the figure), which introduces the minus sign.* We can then obtain

 
mg ( s sub f - s sub i ) sin{ theta } equals 1 over 2 meters ( v sub f to the power of 2 - v sub i to the power of 2 ), 
 v sub f to the power of 2 equals v sub i to the power of 2 + 2g ( s sub f - s sub i ) sin{ theta }. checkmark
 

*What is gained by using the work-energy theorem?*

For a frictionless *plane*, not much. Newton's 2nd law is easy to solve only for this particular case, whereas the work-energy theorem gives the final speed for any shaped frictionless surface. For an arbitrary curved surface, the normal is not constant, where it may be difficult or impossible to solve analytically using Newton's 2nd law.

For motion along a surface, the normal force never does any work because it's perpendicular to the displacement. A calculation using the work-energy theorem avoids this difficulty and applies to more general solutions.

Problem Solving Strategy.

Work-Energy Theorem.

Item 1. Draw a free-body diagram for the object or system of interest. Include only the forces acting on that object or system.

Item 2. Identify the displacement over which the work is done. For each force, decide whether it has a component parallel or antiparallel to the displacement. Forces perpendicular to the displacement do zero work.

Item 3. Calculate the work done by each force, keeping the sign of each contribution. A force that helps the motion does positive work, while a force that opposes the motion does negative work.

Item 4. Add the work contributions to find the net work,

7.4 Power

Power.

Thus far, we have not included how work or energy can change over time. We express the relation between work done and the time interval in doing it by introducing the concept of **power**. Since work can vary as a function of time, we first define **average power** as the work done change in W during a time interval change in t, or

 
P_ ave equals change in W over change in t.
 

We define the **instantaneous power** as the work done dW over an infinitesimal time dt.

Power.

Power is defined as the rate of doing work, or the limit of the average power for time intervals approaching zero,

 
P equals dW over dt.
 

 

If the power is constant over a time interval, the average power for that interval equals the instantaneous power, and the work done by the agent supplying the power is determined as W equals P change in t. If the power during the interval varies with time, then the work is the time integral of the power,

 W equals integral P dt. 

The work-energy theorem relates how work can be transformed into kinetic energy. We can also define power as the rate of transfer of energy. Work and energy are measured in units of joules ( J ), so power is measured in units of joules per second ( J/s ), which has been given the SI name watts ( 1 J/s equals 1 W ). Another common unit for expressing power capability of everyday devices is horsepower: 1 hp equals 746 W.

The power involved in moving a body can also be expressed in terms of the forces acting on it. If a force vector F acts on a body that is displaced by d vector r in a time dt, the power expended by the force is

 
P equals dW over dt equals vector F dot d vector r over dt, 
 equals vector F dot ( d vector r over dt ) equals vector F dot vec v,
 

where vec v is the velocity of the body.
