5.1 Forces

Newton's Laws of Motion.

Chapter roadmap.

 

This chapter introduces Newton's laws of motion as the foundation for connecting forces to changes in motion. Forces describe interactions between objects, and Newton's laws explain how those interactions determine acceleration, equilibrium, and the motion that follows.

As you read, focus on three questions:

Item 1. What forces act on the object or system?

Item 2. What direction does each force point?

Item 3. How does the net force determine the object's acceleration?

These ideas will be used to build free-body diagrams, connect forces to motion, and prepare for more realistic applications in the next chapter.
 

Forces.

The study of motion is called *kinematics*, which describes the way objects move through their velocity and acceleration. 

The study of what *causes objects to move* is called **dynamics**), which describes the motion of objects and systems through forces. The foundation of dynamics are the laws of motion developed by Isaac Newton, although some of the fundamental principles were developed earlier by Galileo Galilei.

The development of Newton's laws marked a transition from Renaissance thinking (i.e., reliance on ancient texts) to Scientific Revolution thinking which developed many of the fundamental pillars of the modern era. Newton's description of mechanics is now described as *classical mechanics* where it applies to most situations until the 20th century. The development of modern physics was prompted by discoveries of subatomic particles which move at speeds comparable to the speed of light. To describe the motions of these particles, we need *quantum mechanics*.

Working Definition of Force.

We need a "working" definition of a force to help build some intuition until we get to the more formal definition given by Newton's 2nd Law. An intuitive definition of a force is

Bullet point. a push or a pull.

A push or a pull has both magnitude (i.e., how hard) and a direction, so it is a vector quantity. Force can be represented by vectors or expressed as a multiple of a standard force.

Our everyday experience gives a good idea of how multiple forces add. If 2 people push in different directions on a third person (see the figure), we might expect the total force to be in the direction of the resultant vector (or force). Forces, like vectors, are represented by arrows.

Drawing Free-Body Diagrams.

 

A free-body diagram is a simple sketch that helps us focus on the forces acting on one object or system. Instead of drawing every detail in the scene, we isolate the object and represent each force with an arrow.

When drawing a free-body diagram:

Item 1. Choose the object or system you want to analyze.

Item 2. Represent the object with a simple shape, such as a point or box. This should be in 1980s style graphics as a point, circle, triangle, or rectangle. Place it at the *origin* of an xy -coordinate system.

Item 3. Draw each force acting on that object as an arrow.

Item 4. Label each force so its physical source is clear.

Item 5. Choose coordinate axes that make the problem easier to describe.

Item 6. Draw a separate free-body diagram for each object in the problem. 

The goal is not to make realistic art. The goal is to make the force relationships clear enough that Newton’s laws can be applied.

Vector Notation for Force.

The SI unit of force is called the **Newton** (abbreviated N ), which is the force needed to accelerate an object with a mass of 1 kg at a rate of 1 meters per second squared (i.e., 1 newtons equals 1 kg dot meters per second squared ). *Note that a small apple has a weight of about 1 newtons.*

We can describe a force in the form

 
 vector F equals a i hat + b j hat, (in 2D) 
 vector F equals a i hat + b j hat + c k hat. (in 3D) 
 

In the figure, let's suppose that ice skater 1 (on the left) pushes horizontally with a force of 30 newtons to the right (or vector F _1 equals 30 i hat N ). Similarly, if ice skater 2 (on the bottom) pushes with a force of 40 newtons in the positive vertical direction, we would write vector F _2 equals 40 j hat N. 

The resultant force causes the third ice skater to accelerate. The **net external force** vector F _ net is found by taking the vector sum of **all external forces** acting on an object or system. We can then represent the net external force (in general) as:

 
 vector F _ net equals sum vector F _ ext, 
 equals vector F _1 + vector F _2 + dot s + vector F _N, 
 equals F sub net,x i hat + F sub net,y j hat + F sub net,z k hat, 
 equals sum sub i equals 1 to the power of N F sub ix i hat + sum sub i equals 1 to the power of N F sub iy j hat + sum sub i equals 1 to the power of N F sub iz k hat.
 

In this example, we have vector F _ net equals vector F _1 + vector F _2 equals 30 i hat + 40 j hat N. To find the magnitude of the net (resultant) force F_ net, we sum the squares of the components and take the square root, or

 F_ net equals square root of (30 newtons ) squared + (40 newtons ) squared equals 50 newtons, 

where the direction is given by

 theta equals tan to the power of -1 ( F sub net,y over F sub net,x ) equals tan to the power of -1 ( 40 over 30 ) equals 53 degrees 

measured from the positive x -axis, as shown in the free-body diagram in Fig. b.

5.2 Newton's 1st Law

Newton's 1st Law.

Newton's 1st law is a restatement of Galileo's principle of **inertia**, which states that an object in motion tends to slow down unless some effort is made to keep it moving. **Newton's 1st law** gives a deeper explanation of this observation.

Newton's 1st Law of Motion.

 

An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion remains in motion with constant velocity, unless acted on by a net external force.
 

Note the expression "constant velocity", which means that the object maintains a path along a straight line (since neither the magnitude nor direction changes). Also the verb "remains" tells us that it keeps on moving (or not moving) as part of the status quo.

Newton's 1st law says that there must be a cause for any change in velocity (i.e., acceleration) to occur. The cause is a net external force. An object sliding across a table or floor slows down due to the net force of friction acting on the object. **If friction disappears, will the object slow down?**

Figure referenced in the course notes.

Consider an air hockey table (the figure). When the air is turned off, the puck slides only a short distance before friction slows it to a stop. However, with the air turned on, it creates a *nearly* frictionless surface, and the puck glides a long distance without slowing down. If we know enough about the friction, we can accurately predict how quickly the object slows down.

Checkpoint.

Newton's 1st law is general and can be applied to anything from an object sliding on table, a satellite in orbit, or even blood pumped from the heart. Experiments have verified that any change in velocity (speed or direction) must be caused by an external force.

Aristotle had claimed that objects had a natural state and needed little "angels" to keep the objects moving. It was Galileo who questioned this idea, which led him to ask the fundamental question: "What is the cause?" Thinking in terms of *cause and effect* is fundamentally different from the typical ancient Greek approach. 

Gravitation and Inertia.

Regardless of the scale of an object, two properties remain valid: gravitation and inertia because they are both connected to mass.

Bullet point. **Mass** is a measure of the amount of matter in something. It has volume and takes up space.

Bullet point. **Gravitation** is the attraction of one mass to another. The magnitude of this attraction is your weight, and it is a force.

Mass is related to **inertia**, which is the ability of an object to *resist changes in its motion*, or to resist acceleration. Newton's 1st law is often called the **law of inertia**.

Inertial Reference Frames.

Newton's 1st law can also be stated as *"Every body remains in its state of uniform motion in a straight line unless it is compelled to change that state by forces acting on it."* 

Bullet point. To Newton, "uniform motion in a straight line" meant constant velocity, which includes the special case of zero velocity (or rest).

Therefore the 1st law says that the velocity of an object remains constant if the net force on it is zero, or

 vector F _ net equals sum vector F sub ext equals 0. 

Newton's 1st law is also considered as a statement about reference frames. It provides a method for identifying a special type of reference frame called the **inertial reference frame**.

In principle, we can make the net force on a body zero, if its velocity relative to a given reference frame is constant and then that frame is defined as inertial (recall ). An inertial reference frame is one where Newton's 1st law is valid. 

Inertial Reference Frame.

 

A reference frame moving at constant velocity relative to an inertial reference frame is also inertial. A reference frame accelerating relative to an inertial frame is not inertial.

Bullet point. *Are inertial frames common in nature?*

A reference frame at rest to the most distant (or "fixed") stars is inertial. All frames moving uniformly with respect to this fixed-star frame are also inertial. For example, a nonrotating reference frame attached to the Sun is (for all practical purposes) inertial because its velocity relative to the fixed stars does not vary by more than one part in ten to the 10.

Earth accelerates relative to the fixed stars because it rotates on its axis and *revolves* around the Sun. Hence a reference frame attached to its surface is not inertial. For most problems, such a frame serves as a sufficiently accurate *approximation* to an inertial frame because the acceleration relative to the fixed stars is small ( { approximately }ten to the minus 2 meters per second squared ). Thus we consider reference frames fixed to the Earth as inertial.

No particular inertial frame is more special than any other. All inertial frames are equivalent. In analyzing a problem, we choose one particular inertial frame simply based on convenience.
 

Newton's 1st Law and Equilibrium.

Newton's first law tells us about the *equilibrium* of a system (i.e., vector F _ net equals 0 ). From the figure, the forces combine to form a net external force: vector F _ net equals vector F _1 + vector F _2. To create equilibrium, we require a *balancing force* that will produce a net force of zero.

Bullet point. This force must be equal in magnitude, but opposite in direction to vector F _ net (i.e., vector F _ bal equals - vector F _ net ).

For the ice skaters, we found vector F _ net equals 30 i hat + 40 j hat N, where the balancing force is 

 
 vector F _ bal equals - vector F _ net, 
 equals -30 i hat - 40 j hat N.
 

Newton's 1st law in vector form is given as

 
 vec v equals constant, when vector F _ net equals vector 0 newtons.
 

For the net force to equal zero, the velocity vector vec v of the object is a constant.

Derivative of a Constant (Calculus-framing).

 

From calculus, we have the constant rule which means that for any function f(x) equals c (where c is a constant), the derivative f^ prime(x) equals 0.

*What does this mean for an object moving with constant velocity?* We have loosely defined a force as a push or pull (or causing an object to accelerate). Recall the definition of acceleration as

 vector a equals d vector v ( t) over dt, 

which means the acceleration is the slope of the velocity curve. If vec v (t) equals c, then the slope is equal to zero.

If there is no acceleration, then there is no net force (assuming mass remains constant). This does not mean that no forces act on the object. It means that all external forces acting on the object balance.

The Math.

Newton's first law applies when the net external force on an object is zero. Under that condition, the acceleration is zero, so the object remains at rest or moves with constant velocity,

 
 sum vector F _ ext equals vector 0 therefore vector a equals vector 0.

 sum vector F _ ext equals vector N + vector W,

The total net force is zero, so Newton's first law applies in this case as well.

---

For the car moving at constant velocity, the vertical forces balance and the horizontal driving and resistive forces also balance. The important point is that zero net force means zero acceleration, not necessarily zero velocity.

5.3 Newton's 2nd Law

Newton's 2nd Law.

Newton's 2nd law gives the cause-and-effect relationship between force and changes in motion. It is quantitative and used extensively to calculate what happens in situations involving a force.

Force and Acceleration.

*What do we mean by a change in motion?*

A change in motion is equivalent to a change in velocity (i.e., an acceleration). Newton's 1st law says that a net external force *causes* a change in motion, and thus, we see that a *net external force causes nonzero acceleration*.

Bullet point. An external force is one that is outside the system of interest. In Fig. a, the system of interest is the car plus the person within it. The two forces exerted by the students outside are external forces.

Bullet point. An internal force acts between elements of the system. Thus the force the person exerts holding onto the steering wheel is an internal force. 

Figure referenced in the course notes.

Only external forces affect the motion of a system, according to Newton's 1st law. Therefore, we must define the boundaries of the system to separate the internal from external forces.

In Fig. a, there are arrows denoting the external forces present:

Bullet point. The weight vector w includes the car + person inside.

Bullet point. The car is supported by the ground vector N, which will cancel the weight, since the car is not floating nor sinking.

Bullet point. The friction f resists the motion and must be oriented opposite (to the left).

Bullet point. The forces from each student pushing ( vector F _1 and vector F _2 ) are applied to accelerate the car to the right.

All the forces add together to produce the net force vector F _ net (see Fig. b). The free-body diagram (Fig. a) shows all of the forces acting on the system, where the dot at the center represents the center-of-mass. Forces in the same direction are shown collinearly.

In Fig. c, a larger net external force produces a larger acceleration ( vector a ^ prime > vector a ) when the tow truck pulls the car. The acceleration is proportional to and in the same direction as the **net external force** acting on a system. Mathematically this is written as

 vector a is proportional to vector F _ net, 

where the symbol is proportional to (or ` is proportional to `) means "proportional to." 

Once the system of interest is chosen, identify the external force and ignore the internal ones. It is a huge simplification to disregard the numerous internal forces acting between objects within the system. This simplification helps us solve some complex problems.

Recall that inertia is the "resistance to motion" and is related to an objects mass. Therefore it seems reasonable that a more massive object requires more acceleration for it to move. 

In the figure, the same net external force applied to a basketball produces a much smaller acceleration when it is applied to an SUV.

Figure referenced in the course notes.

Therefore, we can write the proportional relationship between acceleration and mass as

 a is proportional to 1 over m, 

where m is the mass of the system and a is the *magnitude* of the acceleration. Combining the two proportionalities yields **Newton's 2nd law.**

Newton's 2nd Law of Motion.

 

The acceleration of a system is 

Bullet point. directly proportional to the external force acting on the system, 

Bullet point. in the same direction as the external force acting on the system, and 

Bullet point. inversely proportional to its mass.

In equation form, Newton's 2nd law is

 
 vector a equals fraction { vector F _ net } meters, 
 vector F _ net equals sum vector F equals m vector a.
 

Since the mass m is a scalar, we can see that it scales the vector vector a to give the net external force vector F _ net. Also we can use the properties of vectors to write the scalar equivalent as

 
F_ net equals ma.

Mass and Weight.

Mass and weight are often used interchangeably in everyday conversation. In physics, however, there is an important distinction. Weight is the pull of gravitational force on an object, where it depends on the distance to the center of an object.

Bullet point. Weight is the gravitational force acting on an object, so it does vary depending on gravity. 5.5 Newton's 3rd Law

Newton's 3rd Law.

Newton's 3rd Law of Motion.

 

For every interaction between two objects, the forces come in pairs. If object A exerts a force on object B, then object B exerts a force on object A with equal magnitude and opposite direction.

Using vector F sub AB to mean “the force on B by A ” and vector F sub BA to mean “the force on A by B,” Newton’s third law is

 
 vector F sub AB equals - vector F sub BA.
 

These two forces act on different objects, so they do not cancel in a free-body diagram of a single object.
 

Newton's 3rd law represents a certain *symmetry* in nature: Forces always occur in pairs, and one body cannot exert a force on another without experiencing a force itself. We sometimes refer to this law loosely as "action-reaction".

Consider a swimmer pushing off the side of a pool (see the figure). She pushes against the pool wall with her feet and accelerates in the opposite direction of her push. The wall has exerted an equal and opposite force on the swimmer.

Figure referenced in the course notes.

You might think that two equal and opposite forces would cancel, but they do not because *they act on different systems.* In this case, there are two systems that we could investigate: (a) the swimmer and (b) the wall.

Bullet point. (a) If we select the swimmer, then vector F _ wall on feet is an external force on this system and affects the swimmer's motion. The swimmer moves in the direction of this force. F_ feet on wall 

Bullet point. (b) The force vector F _ feet on wall acts on the wall and thus, it does not directly affect the motion of the swimmer and does not cancel vector F _ wall on feet.

The swimmer pushes in the direction opposite that in which she wishes to move. The reaction to her push is thus in the desired direction. In a free-body diagram, we never include both forces of an action-reaction pair, we only use vector F _ wall on feet, not vector F _ feet on wall.

Other examples of Newton's 3rd law are easy to find:

Bullet point. A car accelerates forward because the ground pushes forward on the drive wheels, in reaction to the drive wheels pushing backward on the ground.

Bullet point. Rockets move forward by expelling gas backward at high velocity. This reaction force is called **thrust**, which pushes a body forward in response to a backward force.

Bullet point. Helicopters create lift by pushing air down, thereby experiencing an upward reaction force.

There are two important features of Newton's 3rd law.

Item 1. The forces exerted (action and reaction) are always equal in magnitude but opposite in direction.

Item 2. These forces are acting on different bodies or systems: A 's force acts on B and B 's force on A. They don't act on the same body and thus, they do not cancel each other.

5.6 Common Forces

Common Forces.

Normal Force.

Weight is a pervasive force that acts at all times and must be counteracted to keep an object from falling (i.e., in static equilibrium). You must support the weight of a heavy object by pushing up on it when you hold it stationary.

When a bag of dog food is placed on the table (see the figure), the table sags slightly under the load. This is more noticeable on a card table, compared to a sturdy oak table. Unless an object is deformed beyond its limit, *it will exert a restoring force* like a spring. The greater the deformation, the greater the restoring force. The next external force on the load is zero, when the load is stationary on the table. 

Figure referenced in the course notes.

Whatever supports a load must supply an upward force *equal* to the weight of the load. If the force supporting the weight of an object is perpendicular to the surface of contact, this is defined as a **normal force** vector N. This means that the normal force experienced by an object resting on a horizontal forced can be expressed in vector form and scalar form as:

 
 vector N equals m vector g, 
N equals mg.
 

Note.

Do not confuse the symbol N for the normal-force magnitude with the unit newton, N. The normal force is not automatically equal to the object’s weight. It equals whatever value is required by the force balance perpendicular to the surface. For an object at rest on an incline, the normal force balances only the perpendicular component of the weight.
 

When an object rests on an incline that makes an angle theta with the horizontal, the force of gravity acting on the object is divided into two components:

Item 1. a force acting perpendicular to the plane, w sub y, and

Item 2. a force acting parallel to the plane, w sub x.

The normal force vector N is typically equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to w sub y, where the component parallel to the plane w sub x causes the object to accelerate down the incline (see the figure). The w sub x is the unbalanced force which allows the acceleration to occur, where w sub y is balanced by the normal force N.

Figure referenced in the course notes.

5.7 Drawing Free-body Diagrams

Drawing Free-body Diagrams.

The first step in describing and analyzing most phenomena in physics involves the careful drawing of a free-body diagram (FBD). FBDs have been used throughout this chapter. A FBD must only include the external forces acting on the body of interest.

Once we have drawn an accurate FBD, we can apply 

Bullet point. Newton's 1st law if the body is in equilibrium ( F_ net equals 0 ), or

Bullet point. Newton's 2nd law if the body is accelerating ( F_ net is not equal to 0 ).

Checkpoint.

**MIT OpenCourseWare**.

 

 
 

Constructing Free-Body Diagrams.

 

Observe the following rules when constructing a FBD:

Item 1. Draw the object under consideration as a simpler shape (e.g., point, box, etc.). It does not have to be artistic. We often place the object at the origin of an xy -coordinate system.

Item 2. Include all forces that act on the object, representing these forces as vectors. Do not include the net force on the object. We never include both forces of an action-reaction pair.

Item 3. Convert the FBD into a more detailed diagram showing the x - and y -components of a given force. In this case place a squiggly line through the original vector to show that it is no longer in play (i.e., replaced by components).

Item 4. If there are more than a single object in the problem, draw a separate FBD for each object.

If there is acceleration, we do not directly include it in the FBD. However, it may help to indicate acceleration outside the FBD.

The table is treated as frictionless, so no friction force acts on m sub 1. The rope tension has the same magnitude on both blocks.

---

The Math.

To construct the free-body diagrams, each block is isolated and only forces acting on that block are included.

For block m sub 1, the weight vector w _1 acts downward, the normal force vector N from the table acts upward, and the rope tension vector T acts horizontally to the right toward the pulley.

For block m sub 2, the weight vector w _2 acts downward and the rope tension vector T acts upward. The tension has the same magnitude in both diagrams because the rope is massless and the pulley is frictionless.

Apply Newton’s second law separately to each block, using +x to the right for m sub 1 and +y upward for m sub 2.

For block m sub 1, we have for the horizontal motion

 
 sum F sub x equals m sub 1 a 
T equals m sub 1 a.
 

For block m sub 1 in the vertical direction,

 
 sum F sub y equals 0 (no vertical acceleration) 
N - m sub 1 g equals 0 newtons equals m sub 1 g.
 

For block m sub 2, we have for the vertical motion,

 
 sum F sub y equals m sub 2 a sub y 
T - m sub 2 g equals m sub 2 (-a) 
 m sub 2 g - T equals m sub 2 a.
 

Then, we can solve the two acceleration–tension equations for a in terms of T:

 
T equals m sub 1 a 
 m sub 2 g - T equals m sub 2 a.
 

Then, we can solve for a and T separately in terms of the masses and g: 

 
 m sub 2 g - m sub 1 a equals m sub 2 a 
a equals m sub 2 g over m sub 1 + m sub 2, 
T equals m sub 1 ( m sub 2 g over m sub 1 + m sub 2 ) 
 equals m sub 1 meters sub 2 over m sub 1 + m sub 2 g.
 

---

The Conclusion.

The free-body diagram for m sub 1 contains vector N upward, vector w _1 downward, and vector T to the right. The free-body diagram for m sub 2 contains vector T upward and vector w _2 downward. The two blocks require separate free-body diagrams because the forces acting on each block are different, even though their accelerations are linked by the rope.

Because the rope is massless and taut over a frictionless pulley, the tension magnitude is the same on both blocks and the acceleration magnitudes are equal:

 
a equals m sub 2 g over m sub 1 + m sub 2, 
T equals m sub 1 meters sub 2 g over m sub 1 + m sub 2.
