Drawing Free-body Diagrams.

The first step in describing and analyzing most phenomena in physics involves the careful drawing of a free-body diagram (F B D). F B Ds have been used throughout this chapter. A F B D must only include the external forces acting on the body of interest.

Once we have drawn an accurate F B D, we can apply

Newton's first law if the body is in equilibrium ( F net equals 0 ), or

Newton's second law if the body is accelerating ( F net is not equal to 0 ).

Constructing Free-Body Diagrams.

Observe the following rules when constructing a F B D:

Draw the object under consideration as a simpler shape (e.g., point, box, etc.). It does not have to be artistic. We often place the object at the origin of an x-y coordinate system.

Include all forces that act on the object, representing these forces as vectors. Do not include the net force on the object. We never include both forces of an action-reaction pair.

Convert the F B D into a more detailed diagram showing the x- and y-components of a given force. In this case place a squiggly line through the original vector to show that it is no longer in play (i.e., replaced by components).

If there are more than a single object in the problem, draw a separate F B D for each object.

If there is acceleration, we do not directly include it in the F B D. However, it may help to indicate acceleration outside the F B D.

Example Problem: Two Blocks on an Inclined Plane.

The Problem.
Construct the free-body diagram for object A and object B in the figure.

The worked solution follows.

The Model.

Objects A and B are treated as separate particles on an inclined plane. The x axis is chosen parallel to the incline, and the y axis is chosen perpendicular to the incline. Object A is attached to a cord and is in contact with object B. Object B is in contact with both object A and the incline. Normal forces act perpendicular to contact surfaces, friction forces act parallel to contact surfaces, and tension acts along the cord. Each free-body diagram includes only forces acting on the isolated object.

The Math.

For object A, the forces acting on the isolated object are its weight vector w sub A, the tension vector T from the cord, the normal force vector N sub BA exerted by B on A, and the friction force vector f sub BA exerted by B on A.

For object B, the forces acting on the isolated object are its weight vector w sub B, the normal force vector N sub B from the incline, the friction force vector f sub B from the incline, the normal force vector N sub AB exerted by A on B, and the friction force vector f sub AB exerted by A on B.

The contact forces between A and B occur in third-law pairs. The corresponding force equation is

vector N sub AB equals minus vector N sub BA,
vector f sub AB equals minus vector f sub BA.

These pairs act on different objects, so they appear on different free-body diagrams.

The Conclusion.

The free-body diagram for object A contains vector w sub A, vector T, vector N sub BA, and vector f sub BA. The free-body diagram for object B contains vector w sub B, vector N sub B, vector f sub B, vector N sub AB, and vector f sub AB. Drawing the objects separately is essential because action-reaction pairs do not cancel within a single object's free-body diagram.

The Problem.
A force is applied to two blocks in contact, as shown in the figure. Draw the reaction forces between the two blocks.

The worked solution follows.

The Model.

The blocks are treated as two separate systems on a horizontal surface. The positive x direction is chosen to the right, in the direction of the applied force on block m sub one. At the contact surface, block m sub one pushes on block m sub two to the right, while block m sub two pushes on block m sub one to the left. The prompt asks only for the reaction forces between the blocks, so other forces are not required unless drawing complete free-body diagrams.

The Math.

Newton's third law states that the interaction forces between the two blocks have equal magnitude and opposite direction. If vector A sub one two is the force on block m sub two due to block m sub one, and vector A sub two one is the force on block m sub one due to block m sub two, then

vector A sub one two equals minus vector A sub two one.

Because block m sub one pushes block m sub two to the right, vector A sub one two points in the positive i-hat direction. The reaction force vector A sub two one therefore points in the negative i-hat direction.

The Conclusion.

The reaction-force pair is vector A sub one two on block m sub two to the right and vector A sub two one on block m sub one to the left. These forces have equal magnitude and opposite direction, but they act on different blocks, so they do not cancel in either block's individual free-body diagram.

The Problem.
A block rests on the table, as shown in the figure. A light rope is attached to it and runs over a pulley. The other end of the rope is attached to a second block. The two blocks are said to be coupled. Block m sub two exerts a force due to its weight, which causes the system (two blocks and a string) to accelerate.
We assume that the string has no mass so that we do not have to consider it as a separate object. Draw a free-body diagram for each block.

The Model.

The two blocks are treated as separate systems connected by a taut, massless rope divided by a frictionless pulley. For block m sub one, the positive x direction is to the right, toward the pulley, and the positive y direction is upward. For block m sub two, the positive y direction is upward. The table is treated as frictionless, so no friction force acts on m sub one. The rope tension has the same magnitude on both blocks.

The Math.

To construct the free-body diagrams, each block is isolated and only forces acting on that block are included.

For block m sub one, the weight vector w sub one acts downward, the normal force vector N from the table acts upward, and the rope tension vector T acts horizontally to the right toward the pulley.

For block m sub two, the weight vector w sub two acts downward and the rope tension vector T acts upward. The tension has the same magnitude in both diagrams because the rope is massless and the pulley is frictionless.

Apply Newton’s second law separately to each block, using plus x to the right for m sub one and plus y upward for m sub two.

For block m sub one, we have for the horizontal motion

sum F sub x equals m sub one a
T equals m sub one a.

For block m sub one in the vertical direction,

The sum of F sub y equals zero because there is no vertical acceleration. Therefore, N minus m sub one g equals zero, so N equals m sub one g.

For block m sub two, we have for the vertical motion,

sum F sub y equals m sub two a sub y
T minus m sub two g equals negative m sub two a
m sub two g minus T equals m sub two a.

Then, we can solve the two acceleration–tension equations for a in terms of T:

T equals m sub one a
m sub two g minus T equals m sub two a.

Then, we can solve for a and T separately in terms of the masses and g:

m sub two g minus m sub one a equals m sub two a
a equals m sub two g divided by m sub one plus m sub two,
T equals m sub one ( m sub two g divided by m sub one plus m sub two )
equals m sub one times m sub two divided by the quantity m sub one plus m sub two, times g.

The Conclusion.

The free-body diagram for m sub one contains vector N upward, vector w sub one downward, and vector T to the right. The free-body diagram for m sub two contains vector T upward and vector w sub two downward. The two blocks require separate free-body diagrams because the forces acting on each block are different, even though their accelerations are linked by the rope.

Because the rope is massless and taut over a frictionless pulley, the tension magnitude is the same on both blocks and the acceleration magnitudes are equal:

a equals m sub two g divided by m sub one plus m sub two,
T equals m sub one times m sub two g divided by m sub one plus m sub two.
