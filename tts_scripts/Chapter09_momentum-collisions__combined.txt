Linear Momentum.

When using kinetic energy, we see that an object's motion can be described by combining its mass m and velocity squared v squared into a scalar. However, we can also include the direction of motion into a description that combines mass and velocity vec v as a vector.

Like kinetic energy, this quantity is a way of characterizing the "quantity of motion" for an object. **Momentum** (from the Latin word *movimentum*, meaning movement) is represented by the letter p 

Momentum.

 

The momentum p of an object is the product of its mass and its velocity:

 
 vector p equals m vec v.
 

 

Momentum is a vector quantity, which is a property it inherits from the velocity. The momentum is most useful when determining whether an object's motion is difficult to change or easy to change over a short time interval.

Unlike kinetic energy, momentum depends equally on an object's mass and velocity. The average speed of an air molecule at room temperature is { approximately }500 meters per second, with an average molecular mass of 6 times ten to the minus 25 kg, and thus, its momentum is given by

 p_ molecule equals (6 times ten to the minus 25 kg ) (500 meters per second ) equals 3 times ten to the minus 22 [ kg dot meters per second ]. 

For comparison, a typical automobile might have a speed of only 15 meters per second, but a mass of 1400 kg, which gives it a momentum of 

 p_ car equals (1400 kg ) (15 meters per second ) equals 2.1 times ten to the 4 [ kg dot meters per second ]. 

These momenta are different by 27 orders of magnitude!

9.2 Impulse and Collisions

Impulse and Collisions.

If an object's velocity should change (due to the application of a force), then its momentum changes as well. This indicates a connection between momentum and force.

Suppose you apply a force F on a free object over a time interval change in t. 

Bullet point. An increase in force will result in a proportional increase in the change of momentum. 

Bullet point. A larger time spent applying a force will result in a proportional increase in the change of momentum.

A change in an object's motion can be proportional to the magnitude of the force and to the time interval over which the force is applied.

Mathematically, if a quantity is proportional to two quantities, then it is proportional to their product. The product of a force and a time interval is called **impulse**, which is represented by vector J and has units of N dot s.

Impulse.

 

Let vector F (t) be the force applied to an object over some differential time interval dt. The resulting impulse on the object is defined as

 
d vector J equiv vector F (t)dt.
 
 

The total impulse over the interval t sub f - t sub i is given by

 
 vector J equals integral sub t sub i to the power of t sub f d vector J equals integral sub t sub i to the power of t sub f vector F dt.
 

When a force is applied for an infinitesimal time interval dt, then it causes an infinitesimal impulse d vector J, and the total impulse is the sum over all of these infinitesimal impulses.

To calculate the impulse, we need to know the force function F(t) (which we often don't). However, we can use a result from calculus called a *mean value theorem* or

 f(x)_ ave equals 1 over x sub f - x sub i integral sub x sub i to the power of x sub f f(x)dx. 

Applying this to the time-dependent force function, we find

 
 vector F _ ave equals 1 over t sub f - t sub i integral sub t sub i to the power of t sub f vector F (t)dt,
 

which we can rewrite in terms of the impulse as

 
 vector J equals vector F _ ave change in t.
 

Note.

You can calculate the impulse on the object even if you don't know the details of the force as a function of time; you only need the average force.
 

You usually determine the impulse (by measurement or calculation) and then calculate the average force that caused that impulse. To calculate the impulse, we can start from a version of Newton's 2nd law:

 
 vector F (t) equals m vector a (t), 
 vector J equals integral sub t sub i to the power of t sub f vector F dt equals m integral sub t sub i to the power of t sub f vector a dt, 
 equals m [ vec v ( t sub f ) - vec v ( t sub i ) ].
 

For a constant force vector F _ ave equals vector F equals m vector a, which simplifies to

 
 vector J equals m vector a change in t equals m ( vec v _f - vec v _i ), 
 equals m change in vec v.

9.3 Conservation of Linear Momentum

Conservation of Linear Momentum.

Recall Newton's 3rd law: *When two objects of masses m sub 1 and m sub 2 interact, the force that object 2 applies to object 1 is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.* Let

Bullet point. vector F sub 12 equals the force on m sub 1 from m sub 2,

Bullet point. vector F sub 21 equals the force on m sub 2 from m sub 1.

Then, Newton's 3rd law says

 
 vector F sub 12 equals - vector F sub 21, 
 m sub 1 vector a _1 equals - m sub 2 vector a _2.
 

Note.

These two forces do not cancel because they are applied to *different* objects. vector F sub 12 causes m sub 1 to accelerate, and vector F sub 21 causes m sub 2 to accelerate.
 

Although the magnitudes of the forces on the objects are the same, the accelerations are not because the masses (in general) are different. Therefore, the changes in velocity of each object are different:

 d vec v _1 over dt is not equal to d vec v _2 over dt. 

The products of the mass **and** the change in velocity are equal (in magnitude):

 
 m sub 1 d vec v _1 over dt equals - m sub 2 d vec v _2 over dt.
 

Let's assume that the masses of the objects do not change during the interaction. In that case, we can pull the masses inside the derivatives to get:

 
 d over dt ( m sub 1 vec v _1 ) equals - d over dt ( m sub 2 vec v _2 ), 
 d vector p _1 over dt equals - d vector p _2 over dt.
 

This says that the *rate of change for the momentum is the same for both objects.* Individually, the masses and changes of velocity are both different, but the rate of change of the *product* of m and vec v are the same.

This means that during the interaction, both objects have their momentum changed; but those changes are identical in magnitude but opposite in sign. For example,

Bullet point. the momentum of object 1 might increase, which means that

Bullet point. the momentum of object 2 decreases by exactly the same amount.

If the total combined momentum of the two objects never changes, we can write

 
 d vector p _1 over dt + d vector p _2 over dt equals 0, 
 d over dt ( vector p _1 + vector p _2 ) equals 0,
 

which implies that

 
 vector p _1 + vector p _2 equals constant,
 

using the identity that the derivative of a constant is zero.

Figure referenced in the course notes.

As shown in Figure, the total momentum of the system before and after the collision remains the same. Generalizing this result to N objects, we obtain

 
 sum sub i equals 1 to the power of N vector p _i equals vector p _1 + vector p _2 + ldots + vector p _N equals constant.
 

This is the definition of the total (or net) momentum of a system of N interacting objects, along with the statement that the total momentum of a system of objects is constant in time or conserved.

Conservation Laws.

 
If the value of a physical quantity is constant in time, we say that the quantity is conserved.
 

Requirements for Momentum Conservation.

A system must meet two requirements for it momentum to be conserved:

Item 1. *The mass of the system must remain constant **during** the interaction.* 
 As the objects interact, they may transfer mass from one to another; but any mass one object gains is balanced by the loss of that mass from another. The total mass of the system of objects remains unchanged as time passes: 

 [ dm over dt ]_ system equals 0. 

Item 2. *The net external force on the system must be zero.* 
 As the objects collide, explode, or move around, they exert forces on each other. All of these forces are *internal* to the system, and thus each of these internal forces is balanced by another internal force equal in magnitude and opposite in sign.

 As a result, the change in momentum caused by each internal force is cancelled by another momentum change that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. Therefore, internal forces cannot change the total momentum of a system because the changes sum to zero.

 However if there is some external force that acts on all the objects (e.g., gravity or friction), then this force changes the momentum of the system as a whole. Thus for the momentum of the system to be conserved, we must have

 vector F _ ext equals vector 0. 

A system of objects that meets these 2 requirements is a **closed system** (also called an *isolated* system). 

Law of Conservation of Momentum.

The total momentum of a closed system is conserved:

 sum sub i equals 1 to the power of N vector p _i equals constant. 
 

Along with the conservation of energy, the **Law of Conservation of Momentum** is one of the foundations upon which all physics stands. All our experimental evidence supports this statement. *In a closed system, the total momentum never changes.*

Billiard balls on a table have an external force acting on them (i.e., their weight), but the weights are balanced (or cancelled) by the normal forces so there is *no* net force.

The Meaning of 'System'.

A mechanical system is the collection of objects in whose motion (kinematics or dynamics) interests you. If you are analyzing the bounce of a ball on the ground, you are probably only interested in the motion of the ball, and not of the Earth; thus, the ball is your system.

Consider that you are analyzing a car crash, where the two cars together compose your system (see Figure )

Figure referenced in the course notes.

Problem-Solving Strategy.

 

Using conservation of momentum requires four basic steps: 

Item 1. Identify a closed system (total mass is constant, no net external force acts on the system).

Item 2. Write down an expression representing the total momentum of the system **before** the "event" (explosion or collision).

Item 3. Write down an expression representing the total momentum of the system **after** the "event."

Item 4. Set these two expression equal to each other, and solve for the desired quantity.

9.4 Types of Collisions

Types of Collisions.

Although momentum is conserved in all interactions, not all interactions are the same. The possibilities include:

Bullet point. A single object can **explode** into multiple objects.

Bullet point. Multiple objects can **collide and bounce off** each other (i.e., an *elastic* collision) resulting in the same kinetic energy of the system before and after the collision.

Bullet point. Multiple objects can **collide and merge together** (i.e., an *inelastic* collision) resulting in kinetic energy loss in the system.

Explosions.

The first possibility is that a single object may break apart into 2 or more pieces. An example of this is a firecracker, or a rocket rising through the air toward space. These can be difficult to analyze if the number of fragments after the collision is more than about 3 or 4. Nevertheless, the total momentum of the system before and after the explosion is identical.

If the object is initially motionless, then the system has no momentum and no kinetic energy. After the explosion, the net momentum of all the pieces of the object must sum to zero (since the momentum of this closed system cannot change). However, the system will have a great deal of kinetic energy after the explosion.

Thus, the momentum of the system is conserved in an explosion, but the kinetic energy is definitely not, where it increases. One object becoming many, with an increase of kinetic energy of the system is called an **explosion**.

*Where does the energy come from? Does the conservation of energy still hold?* Yes; some form of potential energy is converted to kinetic energy. In the case of gunpowder burning and pushing out a bullet, chemical potential energy is converted to the kinetic energy of the bullet and the recoiling of the gun.

Inelastic.

The second possibility is the reverse: that 2 or more objects collide with each other and stick together forming one single composite object after the collision. The total mass of this composite object is the sum of the masse of the original objects, and the new single object moves with a velocity dictated by the conservation of momentum.

Although the total momentum of the system of objects remains constant, the kinetic energy doesn't stay constant, but decreases. This type of collision is called **inelastic**.

Any collision where the objects stick together will result in the maximum kinetic energy loss (i.e., K sub f will be a minimum). Such a collision is called **perfectly inelastic**.

In the extreme case, multiple objects collide, stick together, and remain motionless after the collision. Since the objects are all motionless after the collision, the final kinetic energy is also zero; therefore, the loss of kinetic energy is a maximum.

Elastic.

If 2 or more objects approach each other, collide, and bounce off each other, moving away from each other at the same relative speed at which they approached each other. In this case, the total kinetic energy of the system is conserved and the interaction is called **elastic**.

The following gives an overview of the inelastic and elastic collisions by the initial and final kinetic energy K sub f and K sub i:

9.5 Collisions in Multiple Dimensions

Collisions in Multiple Dimensions.

It is more common for collisions to occur in 2D, where the initial velocity vectors can be decomposed into components. Recall that momentum is relate to force through the relation:

 vector F equals d vector p over dt. 

Therefore, both the momentum and force can be written in their component forms together as

 
 F sub x equals d p sub x over dt, 
 F sub y equals d p sub y over dt, 
 F sub z equals d p sub z over dt.
 

Remember, these equations are Newton's 2nd law in vector and component form. Since Newton's 2nd law is true in each direction individually, it follows that conservation of momentum is true in each direction independently, via Newton's 3rd law.

We can now write down the expression for conservation of momentum following the same procedure (i.e., decompose it into components):

 
 p sub x to the power of f equals p sub 1x to the power of i + p sub 2x to the power of i, 
 p sub y to the power of f equals p sub 1y to the power of i + p sub 2y to the power of i.

Problem-Solving Strategy.

Conservation of Momentum in 2D.

THe method for solving a 2D (or even 3D) conservation of momentum problem is generally the same as the method for solving a 1D problem, except that you have to conserve momentum in each dimension simultaneously:

Item 1. Identify a closed system. 

Item 2. Write down the equation that represents the conservation of momentum in each direction, and solve it for the desired quantity for the respective direction.

Item 3. If you are calculating a vector quantity, use the Pythagorean theorem to calculate its magnitude.

9.6 Center of Mass

Center of Mass.

When we describe how an object moves in a way that obeys Newton's 2nd law, we must remember that all objects are actually made of constituent particles (i.e., not a single piece). There are many different types of particles, and they are generally not distributed uniformly in the object.

An extended object might change shape as it moves (e.g., a water ballon or a cat falling). This implies that the constituent particles are applying internal forces on each other, in addition to the external force that is acting on the object as a whole.

The problem is to determine what part of an extended object is obeying Newton's 2nd law when an external force is applied. We also need to determine how the motion of the object as a whole is affected by both the internal and external forces.

**Khan Academy**.

 

 
The center of mass is the mass-weighted average position of a system. As you watch, notice how heavier objects pull the center of mass closer to themselves. This same idea applies whether the system is two particles, the Earth–Moon system, or a complicated object made of many pieces.
 

Internal and External Forces.

Suppose we have an extended object of mass M, composed of N interacting particles. We specify their masses as m sub j, where j equals 1, ldots,N and the total mass M is given as

 
 
M equals sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N m sub j.
 

If we apply some net external force vector F sub ext on the object, every particle experiences some "share" or fraction of the external force. Let:

 vec f _j^ ext equals the fraction of the external force the j th particle experiences. 

In general, the fractions are not equal and can be written as

 vec f _1^ ext is not equal to vec f _2^ ext is not equal to ldots is not equal to vec f _N^ ext. 

We also assume that each particle making up our object can interact with every other particle of the object. Since these result of the particles acting on other particles of the same object, we call them **internal forces** f sub j ^ int. Thus we can have 

 vec f _j^ int equals the net internal force that the j th particle experiences. 

The *net force* (internal + external) on the j th particle is the vector sum, or

 
 vec f _j equals vec f _j^ int + vec f _j^ ext.
 

As a result of the fractional force, the momentum of each particle is changed:

 
 vec f _j equals d vector p _j over dt, 
 vec f _j^ int + vec f _j^ ext equals d vector p _j over dt.
 

The net force vector F on the *object* is the vector sum of these forces:

 
 vector F _ net equals sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N ( vec f _j^ int + vec f _j^ ext ), 
 equals sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N vec f _j^ int + sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N vec f _j^ ext, 
 equals sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N d vector p _j over dt.
 

Remember that each vec f _j^ int is the force on the j th particle from the other particles in the object. By Newton's 3rd law, for every one of these forces, there must be another force with this magnitude, but in the opposite direction. These forces do not cancel. But when all the internal forces are added up, the internal forces must cancel in pairs. It follows that the sum of the internal forces must be zero:

 sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N vec f _j^ int equals 0. 

For the external forces, this summation is simply the total external force that was applied to the whole object:

 sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N vec f _j^ ext equals vector F _ ext. 

As a result,

 
F_ ext equals sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N d vector p _j over dt.
 

This tells us that the total change in momentum of the entire object is due only to the external forces. The internal forces do not change the momentum of the object as a whole. 

Force and Momentum.

Our goal is to determine the equation of motion for the entire object. Let's define the the momentum for a single point particle that represents the total momentum of the system, vector p _ CoM, at the center-of-mass CoM. Then we have

 vector p _ CoM equiv sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N vector p _j, 

and the net external force applied on the system is written as

 
 vector F _ ext equals d vector p _ CoM over dt.
 

The change of momentum is caused by only the net external force, so we can safely drop the ext subscript.

We have found Newton's 2nd law, but for an extended object. This means we are allowed to simplify a system of { approximately }ten to the 23 particles (a mole in chemistry terms) down to describing the motion of a single particle at the center-of-mass CoM.

Center of Mass: Discrete Objects.

Our next task is to find where in the system to place the single particle so that it obeys Newton's 2nd law like we expect (i.e., measure). We can write the Newton's 2nd law using the single particle that contains the mass of the system (Eq. ), or

 
 vector F equals M vector a, 
 equals d vector p _ CoM over dt.
 

We can decompose this description into the one with that describes the momenta of each individual particle, or

 M vector a equals sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N d vector p _j over dt equals d over dt sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N vector p _j, 

which follows because the derivative of a sum is equal to the sum of the derivatives.

If we know the positions of the constituent particles as vector r _j equals ( x sub j, y sub j, z sub j ), then we can write the momentum of a single particle as

 vector p _j equals m sub j vec v _j equals m sub j d vector r _j over dt. 

Using this expression in Newton's 2nd law gives

 
M vector a equals d over dt sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N m sub j d vector r _j over dt, 
 equals d squared over dt squared sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N m sub j vector r _j.
 

We can find the acceleration of the extended object (i.e., center-of-mass) as

 
 vector a equals d squared over dt squared ( 1 over M sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N m sub j vector r _j ).
 

Thus, the point in the object that traces out the trajectory dictated by Newton's 2nd law is inside the parentheses.

Notice that the expression in parentheses can be expressed as a mass-weighted arithmetic mean of the position vectors vector r _j or

 bar{r} equals sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N m sub j vector r _j over M equals sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N ( m sub j over M ) vector r _j equals sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N w sub j vector r _j. 

This is actually called the **center-of-mass** of the object, where it's formally defined as

 
 vector r _ CoM equiv 1 over M sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N m sub j vector r _j.
 

Note.

The center-of-mass does not tell you where the mass is centrally located. In fact, you can have a hollow steel sphere with a vacuum inside it (i.e., no mass inside) and the center-of-mass still lies at the center of the sphere. The actual mass is spread uniformly across its surface. Another (more tasty) example is a doughnut.

r_ CoM,x equals 1 over M sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N m sub jx _j, r_ CoM,y equals 1 over M sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N m sub jy _j, r_ CoM,z equals 1 over M sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N m sub jz _j,
 

and thus, we can write the center-of-mass vector vector r _ CoM and magnitude r_ CoM as:

 
 vector r _ CoM equals r_ CoM,x i hat + r_ CoM,y j hat + r_ CoM,z k hat,

 vec v _ CoM equals d over dt ( 1 over M sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N m sub j vector r _j ) equals 1 over M sum sub j equals 1 to the power of N m sub j vec v _j,
 

which has v sub x -, v sub y -, and v sub z -components.

Problem-Solving Strategy.

Calculating the Center-of-Mass.

The center-of-mass is a position vector. Follow these steps to calculate it:

Item 1. Define your coordinate system.

Item 2. Determine the location of each particle in x, y, and z coordinates relative to a reference location (usually the origin).

Item 3. Determine the mass of each particle, and the total (summed) mass (including any mass at the origin).

Item 4. Calculate the r_ CoM,x, r_ CoM,y, and r_ CoM,z coordinates.
If required, use the Pythagorean theorem to determine its magnitude.

The Model.

---

The Math.

With r sub E equals 0, the center-of-mass location measured from Earth’s center is

 
R equals m sub E r sub E + m sub M r sub M over m sub E + m sub M, 
 equals m sub M r sub M over m sub E + m sub M.
 

This can be written as a linear combination of the radii r sub E and r sub M, where the “coefficients” are mass ratios:

 
R equals ( m sub E over m sub E + m sub M ) r sub E + ( m sub M over m sub E + m sub M ) r sub M.
 

Since r sub E equals 0, the first term drops out, but writing it this way makes the structure of the center-of-mass formula clear.

We now use the observed scale of the Earth–Moon separation relative to Earth’s radius. A commonly used approximate ratio is r sub M is approximately equal to 60 R sub E (equivalently R sub E / r sub M is approximately equal to 0.017 ). Using this ratio gives

 
R is approximately equal to ( 1 over 82 )(60 R sub E ),

Because 0.73 R sub E < 1 R sub E, the Earth–Moon center of mass lies *inside Earth*.

If we also compute the numerical value using appendix data ( m sub E equals 5.97 times ten to the 24 kg, m sub M equals 7.36 times ten to the 22 kg, r sub M equals 3.82 times ten to the 8 meters ), we obtain

The Conclusion.

The key idea is that this conclusion does not require knowing the absolute mass or size of Earth or Moon. It follows from two observable ratios: the mass ratio m sub M / m sub E is approximately equal to 1/81 and the distance ratio r sub M / R sub E is approximately equal to 60. The distance ratio can be determined by angular observations and parallax, and the mass ratio can be inferred from orbital motion, so the essential reasoning is accessible from observation-based astronomy.

9.7 Rocket Propulsion

Rocket Propulsion.

Now we deal with the case where the *mass* of an object is *changing*. As a rocket lifts off, it burns fuel gases that cause a change to its velocity (and hence its momentum). The acceleration is in the opposite direction of the velocity of the ejected fuel. 

Consider a fully fueled rocket ship in deep space that has a total mass m sub o (mass includes both the ship and the fuel). 

Bullet point. At some moment in time, the rocket has a velocity vec v and mass m (mass includes the ship and only the remaining fuel).

Bullet point. The rocket accelerates by burning the fuel it carries and ejecting the burned exhaust gases.

*If the burn rate of the fuel and the velocity at which the exhaust is ejected is constant,* **what is the change of velocity of the rocket as a result of burning all of its fuel?**

**The Organic Chemistry Tutor**.

 

 
Rockets accelerate by throwing mass backward. As you watch, focus on how the expelled fuel carries momentum in one direction while the rocket gains momentum in the opposite direction. The important point is that a rocket does not need air to push against; it accelerates by exchanging momentum with its own fuel.
 

Physical Analysis.

Here's a more detailed description in physical terms:

Bullet point. The rocket engines are continuously ejecting gases, which have some momentum due to their mass and velocity. By conservation of momentum, the rocket's momentum changes by this same amount (with the opposite sign). 

Bullet point. The rate of change of the momentum is constant because the rocket is burning fuel at a constant rate.

Bullet point. From vector F equals d vector p /dt, there is a constant force on the rocket.

Bullet point. As time goes on, the mass of the rocket continuously decreases because of the ejecting gases. Even though the force on the rocket is constant, the resulting acceleration is not. It is continuously increasing, vector a equals vector F /m.

Bullet point. the total change in the rocket's velocity will depend on the amount of fuel mass burned and that dependence is not linear.

The problem has the mass and velocity of the rocket changing, along with the total mass of the ejected gases. Let's define our system to be the rocket + fuel, then this is a closed system (since there are not external forces in deep space). As a result, momentum is conserved for this system (see the figure) and we can apply the conservation of momentum to answer the question.

Figure referenced in the course notes.

The total instantaneous rocket mass is m (i.e., the mass of the ship + the amount of fuel at time t ). We define the rocket's instantaneous velocity as vec v equals v i hat, where the velocity is measured relative to an inertial reference system (i.e., some star that is barely moving relative to the Galactic center). Thus the initial momentum of the system is

 vector p to the power of i equals mv i hat. 

The rocket's engines are burning fuel at a constant rate and ejecting exhaust gases in the - i hat direction. During an infinitesimal time dt, the engines eject a (positive) infinitesimal mass of gas d m sub g at velocity vec u equals -u i hat; note that the exhaust gas velocity is measured with respect to the moving rocket. The gas speed measured relative to the star is (v-u) i hat.

As a consequence of the ejection of the fuel gas, the rocket's mass decreases by d m sub g and it velocity increases by dv i hat. We can write the new momentum vector p to the power of f after this change as

 
 vector p to the power of f equals vector p _ rocket + vector p _ gas, 
 equals [(m-d m sub g )(v+dv) i hat ] + [d m sub g (v-u) i hat ].
 

Since all vectors are in the same direction, we drop the vector notation. Applying conservation of momentum, we have

 
p to the power of i equals p to the power of f, 
mv equals (m-d m sub g )(v+dv)+ d m sub g (v-u), 
mv equals mv + mdv - d m sub gv - d m sub gdv _ d m sub gv - d m sub gu, 
mdv equals d m sub gdv + d m sub gu.
 

Now, d m sub g and dv are each very small (infinitesimal even); thus their product very, very small and we neglect this term. This is similar to adding a nanometer to a kilometer; it's still roughly a kilometer. We obtain:

 mdv equals d m sub gu. 

Since d m sub g represents an *increase* in the mass of ejected gases, ti must also represent a *decrease* in the mass of the rocket: d m sub g equals -dm. Then we have

 
mdv equals -(dm)u, 
dv equals -u ( dm over m ).
 

Integrating from the initial mass m sub o to the final mass m gives us

 
 integral sub v sub i to the power of v dv equals -u integral sub m sub o to the power of m dm over m, 
v- v sub i equals u natural log of ( m sub o over m ).
 

This result is called the rocket equation and is typically given as a difference equation

 
 change in v equals u natural log of ( m sub o over m ).
 

It was originally derived by the Russian rocket scientist Konstantin Tsiolkovsky in 1897. It gives us the change in velocity that the rocket obtains from burning a mass of fuel that decreases the total rocket mass from m sub o to m.

Problem-Solving Strategy.

Rocket Propulsion.

In rocket problems, the most common questions are finding the change in v due to burning some amount of fuel for some amount of time, or to determine the acceleration a that results from burning fuel.

Item 1. To determine the change in v, use the rocket equation directly.

Item 2. To determine the acceleration, determine the force by using the impulse-momentum theorem, where the rocket equation determines the change in velocity.
