Potential Energy and Conservation of Energy.

Potential Energy of a System.

The work done on an object by gravity (near Earth's surface) is a function only of the displacement change in y (or a height h ). This property allows us to define a different kind of energy for the system, which is called potential energy (in contrast to the kinetic energy).

Potential Energy Basics.

Consider the kicking of a football (see the figure). Ignoring friction and air resistance, we begin the analysis immediately after the kick, or at the start of the projectile motion of the ball.

As the football rises, the work done by the gravitational force on the ball is negative, because the ball's displacement is positive vertically and the gravitational force is negative vertically.

The ball slows down until it reaches its highest point in the motion, thereby decreasing the ball's kinetic energy.

The loss in kinetic energy translates to a gain in the gravitational potential energy of the football-Earth system.

As the football falls toward Earth, the work done on the football is now positive, because the displacement and the gravitational force both point in the same direction (vertically downward).

The ball speeds up, which indicates an increase in kinetic energy.

Therefore, energy is converted from gravitational potential energy back into kinetic energy.

Based on this scenario, we define the difference in potential energy from point A to B as the negative of the work done, or

 change in U sub AB equals U sub B - U sub A equals -W sub AB.

We call this a potential energy difference, not just an absolute potential energy. Thus, we define the potential energy relative to a given position vector r and initial position vector r _o. We do this by rewriting the potential energy function as

 change in U equals U ( vector r ) - U ( vector r _o ).

Note.

The choice of the potential energy at a starting location vector r _o is arbitrary and we choose the most convenient starting position depending on the problem. Even though we made an arbitrary choice, we must stick with it and the rest of the problem must be solved consistently with this assumption.

There are some natural (i.e., well-accepted) choices. For example,

the lowest height in a problem is usually defined as zero potential energy,

or if an object is in space, the farthest point away from the system is defined as zero potential energy.

Then the potential energy with respect to zero at vector r _o is just

 change in U equals U ( vector r ).

As long as there is no friction (or air resistance), the change in kinetic energy of the football equals the negative of the change in gravitational potential energy of the football. This can be generalized to

 change in K sub AB equals - change in U sub AB.

 Systems of Several Particles.

A system of interest could contain several particles. The difference in potential energy of the system is the negative work done by the gravitational or elastic, meaning conservative, forces. The potential energy depends mainly on the initial and final positions of the particles, as well as some parameters that characterize the interaction, such as mass for gravity or spring constant for a Hooke's law force.

Potential energy is a property of the interactions between objects in a chosen system, and not just a property of each object.

Types of Potential Energy.

For each type of interaction in a system, you can label a corresponding type of potential energy. The total potential energy of the system is the sum of the potential energies of all types.

This follows from the additive property of the dot product that we found when computing the work done.

Gravitational Potential Energy Near Earth's Surface.

Consider one or more particles near Earth's surface. The gravitational force on each particle is just its weight, m g, near the surface acting vertically down.

According to Newton's third law, a particle exerts a force on Earth of equal magnitude but in the opposite direction.

Newton's second law tells us that the magnitude of acceleration is measured weight divided by the mass, which is g.

Since the ratio of the mass of any ordinary object to Earth's mass is vanishingly small, the motion of Earth can be completely neglected. Therefore, we consider the particle, or a system of particles, subject to the uniform gravitational force of Earth.

The work done on a body by Earth's uniform gravitational force near the surface depends on the mass of the body m, the acceleration due to gravity g, and the difference in height h the body traveled. By definition, this work is the negative of the difference in the gravitational potential energy, or

delta U sub grav equals negative W sub grav, A B,
which equals m g times the quantity y sub B minus y sub A.

We define the gravitational potential energy function near Earth's surface as

U of y equals m g y plus a constant.

You can choose the value of the constant, where the most convenient constant to choose is zero for when y equals zero. This corresponds to the lowest position in the problem.
Example Problem: Gravitational Potential Energy of a Hiker.

With this choice, the constant of integration is determined by the reference condition at the base.

 Elastic Potential Energy.

The work done by a perfectly elastic spring in one dimension depends only on the spring constant k and the squares of the displacements from the unstretched position, x squared minus x naught squared. This work involves only the properties of a Hooke's law interaction and not the properties of real springs and whatever is attached to them.

We define the difference of elastic potential energy for a spring force as the negative of the work done by the spring force. Thus,

delta U equals negative W sub A B,
which equals one-half k times the quantity x sub B squared minus x sub A squared,

where the object attached to the spring travels from point A to point B. The potential energy function corresponding to this difference is

U of x equals one-half k x squared plus a constant.

If the spring force is the only force, it is simplest to take the zero of potential energy at x equals zero. This corresponds to when the spring is at its unstretched length.

Example Problem: Spring Potential Energy.
The Problem.
A system contains a perfectly elastic spring, with an unstretched length of 20 cm and a spring constant of 4 newtons/cm. (a) How much elastic potential energy does the spring contribute when its length is 23 cm? (b) How much more potential energy does it contribute if its length increases to 26 cm?
The worked solution follows.
The Model.
The system consists of a single ideal spring that obeys Hooke’s law and stores elastic potential energy when stretched or compressed from its unstretched length. The spring is assumed to be perfectly elastic, with no energy losses, so its elastic potential energy depends only on the magnitude of the displacement from equilibrium. The displacement x is defined as the difference between the current length of the spring and its unstretched length, measured along the axis of the spring. The reference configuration for zero elastic potential energy is the unstretched length. Because the spring constant is given in N/cm, lengths and displacements are treated consistently in centimeters.
The Math.
Elastic potential energy for an ideal spring is given by U equals one-half k x squared, where k is the spring constant and x is the displacement from the unstretched length.

(a) When the spring length is 23 cm, the displacement from the unstretched length is x equals 3 cm. Substituting this displacement into the elastic potential energy expression gives

U
 equals 1 over 2 k x squared
 equals 1 over 2 (4 newtons/cm )(3 cm ) squared
 equals 0.18 joules.

(b) When the spring length increases to 26 cm, the displacement is x equals 6 cm. Substituting this value gives

U
 equals 1 over 2 (4 newtons/cm )(6 cm ) squared
 equals 0.72 joules.

The additional elastic potential energy contributed relative to part (a) is therefore

 change in U
 equals 0.72 joules - 0.18 joules
 equals 0.54 joules.
The Conclusion.
When the spring is stretched to 23 cm, it stores 0.18 joules of elastic potential energy. Increasing the length to 26 cm increases the stored energy to 0.72 joules, which is an additional 0.54 joules. These results follow directly from the quadratic dependence of elastic potential energy on displacement and from maintaining a consistent unit system throughout the calculation.

 Gravitational and Elastic Potential Energy.

A simple system with both gravitational and elastic types of potential energy is a one-dimensional, vertical mass-spring system. Note that the three mass-spring systems shown on the course webpage are snapshots of the same system at three different times, t sub A, t sub B, and t sub C.

Consider the potential energy of the system, including the mass and spring. We need to define the constant in the potential energy function for gravity and the elastic potential energy.

Often, the ground is a suitable choice for when the gravitational potential energy is zero. In this case, the highest point, or when y equals zero, is a convenient location for zero gravitational potential energy.

In this case, this is also where the spring is unstretched, or at the y equals zero position.

If we consider that the total energy of the system is constant, meaning conserved, then the total energy at point A equals the energy at point C, or

K sub A plus U sub A equals K sub C plus U sub C.

The block is placed just on the spring so its initial kinetic energy is zero.

Initially, both the gravitational and elastic potential energy of the system are zero.

Therefore, the initial energy, kinetic plus potential, is equal to zero.

When the block is released from point A, it expands past point B and arrives at point C. Then, its kinetic energy is zero again. But it now has both gravitational and elastic potential energy. Therefore, we can solve for the distance y that the block travels before coming to a momentary stop at point C by first finding the total energy at point A and point C.

K sub A plus U sub A equals K sub C plus U sub C.

We then identify the individual contributions of kinetic and potential energy at point A and point C:

K sub A equals zero, and K sub C equals zero.

U sub A equals zero, and U sub C equals m g y sub C plus one-half k y sub C squared.

Therefore, we can substitute the energies to solve for y sub C:

zero plus zero equals zero plus m g y sub C plus one-half k y sub C squared,

which gives

y sub C equals negative two m g divided by k.
