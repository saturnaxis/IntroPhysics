Bernoulli's Equation.

When a fluid flows into a narrower channel, its speed increases which means that its kinetic energy increases as well. The increased kinetic energy come from the net work don on the fluid to push it into the channel. If the fluid changes vertical position, work is also done on the fluid by the gravitational force.

A pressure difference occurs when the channel narrows, where this difference results in a net force on the fluid because the P times A equals F, and this net force does work. Recall the work-energy theorem,

 W_ net equals change in K equals K - K sub o equals 1 over 2 mv squared - 1 over 2 meters v sub o to the power of 2. 

The net work done increases the fluid's kinetic energy. As a result, the pressure drops in a rapidly moving fluid whether or not the fluid is confined to a tube.

For example, shower curtains have a disagreeable habit of bulging into the shower stall when the shower is on. The high-velocity stream of water and air creates a region of lower pressure inside the shower, whereas the pressure on the other side remains the same (at the standard atmospheric pressure). This pressure difference results in a net force, pushing the curtain inward.

Similarly, when a car passes a semi on the highway, the two vehicles pull toward each other. The reason is the same: The high velocity of the air between the car and the truck creates a region of lower pressure between the vehicles, and they are pushed together by greater pressure on the outside (see Figure ). THis effect was observed as back as the mid-1800s, when it was found that trains passing in opposite directions tipped precariously toward one another.

Energy Conservation and Bernoulli's Equation.

The application of the principle of conservation of energy to frictionless laminar flow leads to a very useful relation between pressure and a fluid's flow speed. This relation is called **Bernoulli's equation**, named after Daniel Bernoulli who published his studies in his book *Hydrodynamica*.

Consider an incompressible fluid flowing through a pipe that has a varying diameter and height (see Figure ). We identify two locations along the pipe using the subscripts 1 and 2 on the respective physical parameters: cross-sectional area, velocity, height, and pressure. We assume that the density at the two points is the same, where the shaded volumes must be equal since the fluid is incompressible.

Since the two points were chosen arbitrarily, we can write Bernoulli's equation more generally as a conservation principle along the flow.

Bernoulli's Equation.

 

For an ideal fluid moving steadily, the pressure and energy density is conserved along a flow line. The conservation relation is given by

 
P + 1 over 2 rho v squared + rho gh equals constant,
 

where P is the pressure, rho is the mass density, v is the speed of the fluid, and h is the height.

In a dynamic situation, the pressures at the same height in different parts of the fluid may be different if they have different speeds of flow.

 

Analyzing Bernoulli's Equation.

If we follow a small volume of fluid along its path, the sum of pressure and energy density remains the same even though various individual quantities may change. Bernoulli's equation is just a convenient statement of the conservation of energy for an *incompressible* fluid in the absence of friction. 

Bernoulli's equation for *static* fluids.

Consider the simple solution where the fluid is static (i.e., v sub 1 equals v sub 2 equals 0 ). Bernoulli's equation in that case is

 P sub 1 + rho g h sub 1 equals P sub 2 + rho g h sub 2. 

We can simplify the equation by measuring the relative height h as the difference between h sub 2 and h sub 1, or h equals h sub 2 - h sub 1. In this case, we get

 P sub 2 equals P sub 1 + rho gh. 

This equation tells us tha the pressure increases with depth for static fluids. As we go from point 1 to point 2 in the fluid, the depth increases by h and the difference in pressure is equal to rho gh. Thus, Bernoulli's equation confirms the fact that pressure change due to the weight of a fluid is rho gh. 

Bernoulli's principle.

Suppose a fluid is *moving* but its depth is constant (i.e, h sub 1 equals h sub 2 ). Under this condition, Bernoulli's equation becomes

 P sub 1 + 1 over 2 rho v sub 1 to the power of 2 equals P sub 2 + 1 over 2 rho v sub 2 to the power of 2. 

Situations in which fluid flows at a constant depth are so common that this equation is often called **Bernoulli's principle**, which is simply Bernoulli's equation for a constant depth. Bernoulli's principle reinforces that pressure drops as the speed increases in a moving fluid: If v sub 2 > v sub 1 in the equation, then P sub 2 must be less than P sub 1 for the equality to hold. It is more easily seen by

 P sub 1 - P sub 2 equals 1 over 2 rho ( v sub 2 to the power of 2 - v sub 1 to the power of 2 ), 

because v squared>0 and P sub 1 > P sub 2 must be true for the left-side to be positive.

The Problem.

Calculate the pressure in the hose, given that the absolute pressure in the nozzle is 1.01 times ten to the 5 newtons per square meter (atmospheric, as it must be) and assuming level, frictionless flow.

The Model.

We model the fluid as incompressible and flowing steadily, so Bernoulli’s principle applies along a streamline. Because the flow is level, there is no change in gravitational potential energy. The pressure and kinetic energy of the fluid therefore trade off as the speed changes between the hose and the nozzle. The pressure at the nozzle is atmospheric because the fluid is exposed to the air.

---

The Math.

We begin with Bernoulli’s principle, which relates the pressure and speed of the fluid at two points along the same streamline. Because the flow is level, the gravitational terms cancel, leaving a relationship between pressure and kinetic energy.

 
 P sub 1 + 1 over 2 rho v sub 1 to the power of 2 equals P sub 2 + 1 over 2 rho v sub 2 to the power of 2.
 

To determine the pressure in the hose, we solve this equation for P sub 1 by isolating it on one side. Subtracting the kinetic energy term at point 1 and adding the kinetic energy term at point 2 gives

 
 P sub 1 equals P sub 2 + 1 over 2 rho v sub 2 to the power of 2 - 1 over 2 rho v sub 1 to the power of 2.
 

Factoring out the common coefficient simplifies the expression to

 
 P sub 1 equals P sub 2 + 1 over 2 rho ( v sub 2 to the power of 2 - v sub 1 to the power of 2 ).
 

The known values are P sub 2 equals 1.01 times ten to the 5 newtons per square meter, rho equals 1000 kilograms per cubic meter, v sub 1 equals 1.96 meters per second, and v sub 2 equals 25.5 meters per second. Substituting these values gives

 
 P sub 1 equals 1.01 times ten to the 5 newtons per square meter + 1 over 2 (1000 kilograms per cubic meter ) [(25.5 meters per second ) squared - (1.96 meters per second ) squared ] 
 equals 1.01 times ten to the 5 newtons per square meter + 500 (650.25 - 3.84 ) 
 equals 1.01 times ten to the 5 newtons per square meter + 3.23 times ten to the 5 newtons per square meter 
 equals 4.24 times ten to the 5 newtons per square meter.
 
---

The Conclusion.

The pressure in the hose is P sub 1 equals 4.24 times ten to the 5 newtons per square meter. This pressure is greater than atmospheric pressure because the fluid is moving more slowly in the hose than in the nozzle. As the fluid accelerates into the narrower nozzle, kinetic energy increases, which requires a decrease in pressure according to Bernoulli’s principle. This result is consistent with the expectation that higher-speed flow corresponds to lower pressure.
